# Follow-ons, intros and staying warm

Most follow-on money comes from people who already own a piece of the company and
have heard from the founder every month. This file is how that happens without a
pitch.

## The ninety-day contact rule

Every investor gets a real contact at least once a quarter, separate from the
monthly update. A call, a coffee, a specific question - something addressed to
them, not to the list.

| Contact type | When | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly update | Every month, same date | Shared |
| One line in the update, addressed | When something matches their expertise | 2 minutes |
| A question you actually want their view on | Quarterly | 15 minutes |
| A milestone note, when it is hit | On event | 5 minutes |
| A call | Quarterly, or when they ask | 30 minutes |

The note that says "you mentioned you had seen this in a portfolio company - we
just hit it" is worth more than a deck, because it proves they were listened to.

## Warming the next round, honestly

- **Share the milestone when it happens**, not when the round starts. "We crossed ₹1 crore ARR this month" in month 14 is a fact; the same sentence in a pitch is a claim.
- **Ask for a view, not money.** "Would you look at our retention curve and tell me whether it is Series A ready?" gets an answer; "would you invest?" gets a deferral.
- **Name the timing early.** "We will look at a round in the September quarter, and I would like your view before then."
- **Never imply a round is open when it is not.** It creates a deadline you cannot hold, and a fund will ask for the data room.

## Intros, asked for and given

| Ask shape | Good | Bad |
|---|---|---|
| Specific | "Two intros to CFOs at D2C brands doing ₹50 crore+" | "Any investor intros?" |
| Justified | "Our retention now looks like <portfolio company>'s at the same stage" | "We are raising" |
| Easy to forward | A two-line blurb the investor can paste | A deck attachment |
| Reciprocal | "Anything I can do for your portfolio companies?" | Nothing |

When you receive an intro, reply within 24 hours, thank the introducer, and tell
them the outcome - including a no. An introducer who never hears back does not
introduce again.

## The pro-rata conversation

- **Know each investor's pro-rata right** and its expiry, from the SHA.
- **Tell them the round is coming 60-90 days out**, with the range, so they can plan their own fund's reserves.
- **Do not treat a pro-rata decision as a vote of confidence.** A fund passing for reserve reasons is normal and is not a signal about the company.
- **Give them the same information as a new lead**, no more and no less.

## Bridge rounds and insiders

If the company needs a bridge:

1. Decide the instrument with the lawyer and CA, not with the investor.
2. Approach the lead first, with the number, the plan and the runway it buys.
3. Offer the same terms to every existing investor of the same class, in writing.
4. Keep the paperwork to what the lawyer drafts, and the timeline honest.
5. Tell the rest of the cap table before they hear it from someone else.

A bridge handled well is a year of trust. A bridge handled quietly is a reputation
problem at the next round.

## Secondary sales

An investor wanting to sell part of their stake, or a founder wanting liquidity:
this role does not advise. Route to the founder, the lawyer and the company
secretary, confirm the SHA's transfer restrictions, and keep the process
documented. Never discuss one shareholder's sale with another.

## When an investor goes quiet

- **Check the pattern**: does this investor reply to anything, or only to updates?
- **Send one direct, short note** with a specific question. Silence to a direct question is information.
- **Do not chase three times.** Two is the limit; a third reads as need.
- **Note it in the CRM** and tell the founder. An investor who has mentally exited is a pro-rata risk at the next round, and the founder should know before the round, not during it.

## Anti-patterns

- **Only contacting investors when raising.** It is the most common and the most expensive mistake in this role.
- **A mass "checking in" mail.** It is a mail nobody answers and it teaches them the relationship is automated.
- **Asking for an intro without the two-line blurb.** You have transferred the work back to them.
- **Treating a pass as a judgement.** Funds pass for reserve, thesis and timing reasons that have nothing to do with the company.
- **Letting an investor learn about a round from the market.** The pro-rata conversation happens before the term sheet, not after.
- **No record of what was offered.** Two years later nobody remembers who offered what help, and the founder asks the wrong person.
