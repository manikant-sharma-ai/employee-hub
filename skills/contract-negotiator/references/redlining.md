# Redlining: writing the words that fix the clause

A redline is not an objection. It is the sentence that should replace the sentence you do not want.

## The four levers

Every problematic clause yields to one of these, in this order of preference:

| Lever | Example | When it works |
|---|---|---|
| Narrow the scope | "any claim" becomes "third-party claims of IP infringement" | Almost always; it is the cheapest win |
| Cap the exposure | "shall indemnify" becomes "shall indemnify up to the fees paid in the preceding 12 months" | Where the risk is real but bounded |
| Add a condition | "on written notice" becomes "on written notice, and only after a 30-day cure period" | Where the other side wants the right more than the timing |
| Delete and rely on law | Remove the clause and let the statute govern | Where the default is already better than the drafted clause |

## Writing the comment

Four parts, one paragraph:

1. **What the clause does** - "This makes the indemnity unlimited and covers our own negligence."
2. **What it costs** - "On a ₹12 lakh contract that is an unbounded exposure."
3. **The replacement wording**, in quotes, as tracked change or a comment.
4. **The fallback**, offered in the same comment: "If a full carve-out is not possible, we can accept the cap at
   12 months of fees."

Offering the fallback in the first pass is what makes a redline a negotiation instead of a demand.

## Replace, do not annotate

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Clause 9 is unclear." | "Clause 9: replace 'may terminate at any time' with 'may terminate for convenience on 30 days' written notice, with fees for work performed payable'." |
| "Liability seems high." | "Clause 11: insert 'aggregate liability under this Agreement shall not exceed the fees paid in the twelve months preceding the claim'." |
| "We cannot accept this." | "Clause 14: delete in its entirety. Our pre-existing IP and tooling are excluded from any assignment." |

## The silent acceptances

Some deviations cost nothing and are not worth the goodwill:

- A confidentiality term of three years instead of two, on ordinary commercial information.
- The other side's governing law, when both are Indian courts and neither party will litigate.
- A notice period of 45 days instead of 30, when the business has no reason to exit quickly.
- Their invoice format, their purchase-order reference, their standard annexure numbering.
- A mutual NDA with a one-way-sounding title, if the operative clauses are mutual.

Say in the summary which ones were accepted silently and why. That list is what stops the founder wondering
whether the review missed something.

## Cross-references and definitions

Half a clause is the commonest redline error:

- **Read the definition** of every capitalised term in the clause - "Fees", "Services", "Confidential
  Information", "Affiliate".
- **Follow the cross-reference** to the schedule or annexure where the real number lives.
- **Check the survival clause**: an indemnity that survives termination for five years outlives the relationship.
- **Check the order of precedence** - which wins between the body, the order form and the SLA.

## The redline pack

1. The marked-up document, tracked changes on, comments addressed to a named person.
2. A one-page summary of the changes and the two that matter most.
3. The fallback position for each, so the other side's counsel can respond without a second round trip.
4. A version number and a date in the filename.

## Anti-patterns

- Fifty comments, which signals that nothing is important.
- Rewriting the other side's drafting style because it is untidy. Untidy and harmless is not a redline.
- Deleting a clause without a replacement where the deletion creates a gap the statute fills badly.
- Sending a redline without the summary, so the counterparty's lawyer has to reconstruct the intent.
- Accepting a tracked change that quietly alters a definition used in six other clauses.
