# Standard positions: the business's own defaults

A standard position is what the business accepts when nobody has argued for anything else. Written down, it makes
every contract review a comparison instead of a mood.

## The ten clauses that carry the risk

| Clause | Standard position | Why it is the default |
|---|---|---|
| Limitation of liability | Mutual, capped at 12 months of fees paid or ₹<x>, whichever is lower | A cap at three months makes the contract uninsurable; an uncapped one makes it un-signable |
| Indemnity | Mutual, limited to third-party IP claims and breach of confidentiality; no indemnity for the other side's negligence | An uncapped indemnity is unlimited liability wearing a different hat |
| IP ownership | The business keeps its pre-existing IP, tools and know-how; deliverables transfer on full payment | Assigning core IP to a client ends the business's ability to sell the same thing twice |
| Payment terms | 30 days from invoice; 45 for large enterprises; never beyond the cash cycle | A 90-day term is an interest-free loan the business cannot fund |
| Termination for convenience | Either side, 30 days' notice, fees for work done | One-sided termination is a client option with no cost to them |
| Auto-renewal | Opt-in, with a 30-day notice window and a calendar reminder owned by a named person | The commonest silent commitment in a small business |
| Confidentiality | Mutual, 2-3 years, with a carve-out for information that becomes public and for legally required disclosure | A perpetual term on ordinary commercial information is unenforceable in spirit and expensive in practice |
| Data protection | Processing terms per DPDP, breach notice within 72 hours, sub-processors named, deletion on exit | An unnamed sub-processor is an unassessed risk |
| Non-compete and non-solicit | Refused for the business; accepted for individuals only if scoped, timed and paid for | A non-compete on a company blocks its next customer |
| Governing law and jurisdiction | Indian law, courts at the business's registered city, arbitration if the counterparty insists | Litigating in another jurisdiction is a cost the business cannot carry |

## Three states, not two

| State | Meaning | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| Within standard | As the business would have drafted it | Nothing; a line in the summary |
| Deviates, acceptable | Worse than standard but priced and tolerable | The summary, as a noted trade with its cost |
| Deviates, must change | Outside what the business can accept | The redline, with replacement wording and a fallback |

Ranking the third column by cost is what turns a fifty-clause document into a three-line conversation.

## Writing the standard when there isn't one

1. **Collect the last five signed contracts**, of any type.
2. **Extract the same ten clauses** from each into a table.
3. **Mark the outlier** in each row and ask the founder why it was accepted - usually the answer is "they sent it
   and we were in a hurry", which is the reason to write the standard.
4. **Write the default, and the reason in one line.** A position without a reason gets abandoned in the first
   negotiation.
5. **Date it and review it every two quarters.** A standard that never changes is a standard nobody is testing.

## Where the standard does not apply

- **Large counterparties with non-negotiable paper** (a cloud platform, a marketplace, a bank). The standard still
  applies to the read - you still price the exposure - but the redline is a waste of a week. Decide this in the
  first ten minutes.
- **Government and tender contracts**, where the terms are statutory. The read becomes a compliance and pricing
  question, and the answer is often "price the risk in or do not bid".
- **Employment and founder agreements**, which belong to `legal-drafter` and a lawyer.

## Anti-patterns

- A standard copied from a template site, with clauses the business would never enforce.
- Comparing a ₹40 lakh enterprise contract to a ₹40,000 SaaS subscription using the same thresholds.
- A standard that lists aspirations ("fair", "reasonable") rather than positions with numbers.
- Refusing a deviation that costs ₹8,000 while accepting one that costs ₹8 lakh, because the first felt wrong.

## Reading a contract against the standard, in fifteen minutes

1. **The structure first** - parties, term, commercial terms in the annexure, termination, law and jurisdiction.
2. **The ten clauses**, in the order above, marking each within standard, acceptable or must change.
3. **The definitions** the marked clauses depend on, because "Fees" and "Services" usually move the number.
4. **The precedence clause**, so it is clear whether the body, the order form or the SLA wins.
5. **The survival clause**, since an indemnity that outlives the contract is the exposure nobody re-reads.

Fifteen minutes gets the must-change list. The pricing of those clauses is `references/risk-in-money.md`; the
words that fix them are `references/redlining.md`.

## Keeping the standard alive

- **One owner**: the founder, with this role maintaining the document.
- **One review every two quarters**, with the last quarter's negotiations read for what was conceded and why.
- **A dated version** in the file name, so a review three months later can say which standard was applied.
- **A short change log** at the foot of the document: what moved, when, and the contract that moved it.
