# The economics of churn, and the one-page read

Churn matters because of what it costs to replace, and the read exists so the founder can act in a minute.

## What churn costs

| Line | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue lost to churn (annualised) | Churned MRR × 12 | ₹1.62L × 12 = ₹19.4L |
| Replacement cost | New customers needed = churned / close rate; × CAC | 33 / 0.25 × ₹18,000 = ₹23.8L |
| Acquisition spend to stand still | Same, in cash | ₹23.8L |
| Margin forgone | Lost revenue × gross margin | ₹19.4L × 0.62 = ₹12.0L |
| Payback impact | CAC / (ARPA × margin) in months | Longer as churn rises |

The sentence that lands with a founder is not "churn is 8%". It is: "we lost ₹19.4 lakh of annual revenue last
month; replacing it at our close rate needs ₹23.8 lakh of new-customer spend, and we are spending ₹9 lakh a
month on acquisition." That is the read.

## The acquisition-versus-retention frame

Use it as a comparison, not a doctrine. Retention usually wins on unit economics, but only where the churn is
fixable:

| Spend | Cost | Effect | When it wins |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fix onboarding | One-off build | Removes the M1 cliff | Whenever M1 drop exceeds 20% |
| A save play | Per customer | 20-40% of exits, measured | When the reason is `price-value` or `relationship` |
| An account owner | Salary | Broad, slow | Above roughly 100 accounts |
| More acquisition | CAC per customer | Fills a leaking bucket | Only after the leak is measured |

A business spending ₹9 lakh a month on acquisition while losing 8% of customers monthly is buying customers to
replace the ones it loses. Say that plainly, once, with the numbers.

## The monthly read - the shape

`assets/churn-read.md` is the template. It is one page and it never grows. Sections, in order:

1. **The number** - logo churn, gross and net revenue churn, the base, the comparison.
2. **The cohort** - the matrix, and the one row that moved.
3. **The reasons** - counted, with revenue, and the change against last period.
4. **The signal** - the early-warning table summary, reds and ambers, and how many are owned.
5. **The play** - the one play to run this month, its cost, its expected value.
6. **The gap** - what could not be verified, and the connector that would fix it.

## Presenting a fall in churn honestly

Before calling a fall a win, check five things:

| Check | Why |
|---|---|
| Did the definition change? | The commonest false improvement |
| Did the mix change? | Losing small customers first makes the rate fall and the revenue worse |
| Did a cohort mature? | An ageing base churns less by construction |
| Did a save play run? | Saves without a control group are not effect |
| Did collection improve? | Failed payments moved to a later month is deferred churn, not avoided churn |

If two or more of these are true, the read says "not yet attributable" and names what would make it so.

## Talking to the founder

| They ask | The answer starts with |
|---|---|
| "Are we losing customers?" | The base and the number, both periods, then the cohort |
| "Why?" | The counted reasons, with the share and the rupees |
| "What should we do?" | One play, its cost, its expected value, and who runs it |
| "Is it the price?" | Only if the coded share says so; otherwise the evidence that contradicts it |
| "Can we forecast next quarter?" | The range, the assumption, and the error of last quarter's forecast |

## Anti-patterns

- A churn deck with fifteen slides: the decision gets made on slide two or not at all.
- Reporting the number without the rupees it costs to replace.
- Blaming product, price and support in equal measure: a read that blames everything changes nothing.
- Comparing to an industry benchmark with no source: use your own last four quarters.
- Hiding a bad month because a save play is running. The read shows both, separately.
- Letting the monthly read become the only artefact: the coded workbook is what makes next month's read possible.
