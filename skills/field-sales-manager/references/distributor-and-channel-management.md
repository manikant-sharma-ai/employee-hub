# Distributor and channel management

In most Indian field businesses the distributor is the balance sheet: their stock is your unsold inventory and
their credit is your receivable. This file is how to read one.

## The distributor's numbers, five of them

| Number | How to compute | Healthy range |
|---|---|---|
| Stock cover | Distributor stock ÷ average weekly secondary sales | 15-25 days; above 45 is a problem |
| Outstanding | Receivables ÷ average monthly primary | 15-30 days against terms |
| Claim ageing | Open claims ÷ monthly claims raised | Under 30 days; above 60 is a dispute |
| Fill rate | Orders fulfilled ÷ orders placed | Above 95% |
| Distributor ROI | (Margin + scheme income) ÷ (stock + receivables) | 12-25% annualised for FMCG |

A distributor whose ROI is under 10% will quietly stop pushing the brand, and no report will say so. That is the
number to compute before blaming the market.

## Reading the stock position

| Primary | Secondary | Stock cover | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Up | Up | Stable | Genuine growth |
| Up | Flat | Rising | Stuffing. The next two months will be dead |
| Up | Down | Rising | Stuffing with returns coming |
| Flat | Up | Falling | Real demand; watch for a stock-out |
| Down | Up | Falling | Under-supply, and the brand is losing shelf |
| Down | Down | Stable | A demand problem, not a supply one |

The most expensive misreading in field sales is a primary number that is going up while secondary is flat. It
looks like a good month, it pays the incentive, and it is a stock-out and a return three months later.

## Stock-out and return risk

| Signal | Threshold | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Stock cover above 45 days | At the distributor | Stop pushing, run a secondary push |
| Stock cover below 7 days at an A outlet | On the shelf | Emergency replenishment, same week |
| Near-expiry stock | 60 days to expiry | Redistribute or plan a return before it is unsaleable |
| Return requests above 2% of primary | Month | Investigate the cause, not the distributor |

## Credit, the quiet killer

| Practice | Rule |
|---|---|
| Terms | Written, per distributor, with a limit |
| Limit | Set from their actual turnover, reviewed quarterly |
| Stop-supply trigger | A stated number of days overdue, applied without exception |
| Post-dated cheques | Common in India; record the date, diarise it, deposit on time |
| Security deposit or bank guarantee | For a new distributor, before the first large order |
| Credit note discipline | Every claim has a number, a date and a reason |

An exception to the stop-supply rule, granted once for a good relationship, becomes the new rule for everyone.

## Evaluating a new distributor

| Question | Why it decides |
|---|---|
| How many outlets do they cover today, by class? | Coverage is the product they sell you |
| Which brands do they carry, and which is their focus? | You will be the fourth brand and get the fourth share of attention |
| What is their godown size and location? | Delivery frequency and stock capacity |
| How many salesmen, and on whose payroll? | Whether your brand gets a dedicated beat |
| What are their credit terms with retailers? | Your working capital, indirectly |
| What is their financial standing? | A distributor who fails takes your receivable with them |
| Will they share secondary sales data? | Without it, you are blind and they know it |
| What is their reputation with two of your existing retailers? | Ask the retailer, not the reference list |

## The distributor agreement, in six clauses

1. Territory, by pin code, exclusive or not.
2. Margin and scheme, stated as a number, with the payment mechanism.
3. Credit limit and terms, and the stop-supply trigger.
4. Secondary reporting: what, how often, in what format.
5. Stock and expiry policy, and who bears the cost of a return.
6. Termination, notice, and what happens to stock and claims.

Flag the clauses; the wording is `legal-drafter`'s.

## Super-stockist and sub-distributor structures

| Structure | When it works | The risk |
|---|---|---|
| Company to distributor to retailer | Dense urban, high frequency | The distributor owns the relationship |
| Company to super-stockist to distributor | Large states, many small towns | Two margins, and two places for stock to hide |
| Company direct to modern trade | Chains, large formats | Different team, different skills |
| Distributor's salesman only | Small towns, low volume | You get no data and no control of the beat |

## The distributor review, monthly, thirty minutes

| Agenda item | The number |
|---|---|
| Primary and secondary | Both, with the gap explained |
| Stock cover | Days, and the near-expiry list |
| Outstanding and ageing | Buckets, and anything past terms |
| Claims | Open, aged, and the reason |
| Coverage | Outlets billed this month against the universe |
| The one decision | A scheme, a return, a credit stop, or a target change |

## Anti-patterns

- **Judging a distributor on primary alone.** It is the number that flatters and the one that hides the stuffing.
- **A margin renegotiated in a crisis.** It sets a precedent across every distributor in the state.
- **Filling a new distributor with stock to hit a launch number.** The return comes back at your cost, plus the
  relationship.
- **No secondary data clause.** Then every read in this file is impossible, and the distributor's version becomes
  the truth.
- **Tolerating an overdue balance because the relationship is old.** The oldest relationship is the one that
  costs the most when it fails.
