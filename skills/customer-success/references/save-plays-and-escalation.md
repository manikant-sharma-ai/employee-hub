# Save plays and escalation

The account is leaving. This file is about finding out why before you spend
anything, and knowing when to let it go.

## First, establish the reason

Ask one question, in this shape: "Something has changed for you. What is it?"

Then shut up for ten seconds. The first answer is polite; the second is the real
one. Write both down verbatim.

| Stated reason | Usually means | The play that works |
|---|---|---|
| "Too expensive" | Value not visible, or budget cut | Rebuild the value case on their numbers, or resize the plan down rather than out |
| "Not using it" | Adoption failure at onboarding, or a champion who left | A 60-day adoption plan with a named owner on their side, and a trainer session |
| "Missing feature" | One blocker, often with a workaround | A dated commitment with a consequence, or an honest no and a bridge |
| "Went with a competitor" | A price or a relationship gap | Ask what the other one showed them; it is the cheapest competitive research you will get |
| "Team changed" | New buyer, no relationship | Restart as a new sale with the usage data as the case |
| "Consolidating vendors" | A procurement decision above your champion | Get to the person consolidating; offer to be the one kept |
| Silence | Nobody owns the decision | Ask directly: "Is this still a priority for you?" and accept the answer |

## The save plan, one page

1. **The account and the stake** - ARR, renewal date, what is lost if it goes.
2. **The reason, in their words**, with the date and who said it.
3. **What we got wrong** - stated plainly, without defensiveness.
4. **The offer** - the fix, the timeline, the owner on our side and on theirs.
5. **The concession ladder**, with the floor: what we will do before what we will give.
6. **The decision date** - the day we know, and who decides.
7. **The walk-away** - the point at which we let it go cleanly.

## The concession ladder

Spend the cheapest thing first.

| Rung | Concession | Cost to you | When it is right |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | An apology with a named owner and a date | Nothing | Always, first |
| 2 | A senior person on the account | Time | When they feel unimportant |
| 3 | Free training, a workshop, an onboarding redo | Hours | When the reason is adoption |
| 4 | A feature commitment with a date | Roadmap | When one blocker is genuinely the issue |
| 5 | A service credit or a month free | Revenue, once | When there was a real failure on your side |
| 6 | A price reduction | Margin, forever | Last, and only with a term or a scope change |
| 7 | Exit with a handover | The account | When the reason cannot be fixed by anything on this list |

Rung six is where accounts learn that threatening to leave works. If you go there,
take something back - a longer term, a case study, a smaller scope.

## Escalation, and when to wake someone

| Severity | Test | Who is told | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| Watch | Health dropped a band, or one silence over 30 days | Account owner | Same week |
| At risk | Stated intent to leave, or a competitor in evaluation | Founder or head of sales | Same day |
| Critical | Contract value over your threshold, or a public complaint | Founder, and the person who signed the original deal | Within hours |
| Reputational | A public post, a review, a legal threat | Founder, and `legal-drafter` if it is legal | Immediately |

Escalation is not blame. It is the fastest way to put the person who can decide in
front of the customer.

## What the executive call sounds like

- Open with the reason, not the relationship: "You told Priya the reporting is not trustworthy. I want to hear it directly."
- No defence in the first five minutes. Notes only.
- One commitment, with a date, that you can keep.
- Ask what would make them stay, and let the answer be "nothing".

A founder call that arrives with a discount instead of a fix confirms the customer's
suspicion that nobody was listening.

## When to let it go

- The reason is structural - the customer's business no longer needs the category.
- The fix costs more than the account is worth, over the remaining term.
- The relationship has become abusive to the team. No account is worth that.
- The customer wants a price the business cannot serve at without losing money on every renewal after.

Letting go cleanly means: a written wind-down, a data export, a billing stop date,
a handover call, and the reason logged. A clean exit produces a referral; a messy
one produces a review.

## Learning from the loss

Every lost account gets one line in `notes/learnings.md` and a reason code:
price, adoption, feature, relationship, consolidation, business change, service
failure. When the same code appears three times, it is not a customer problem, it
is a product or onboarding problem, and it goes to the founder in writing.

## Anti-patterns

- **Leading with a discount.** It confirms the price was arbitrary and sets the anchor for every future renewal.
- **Arguing with the stated reason.** The customer's perception is the reality you have to work with.
- **Saving an account the business cannot serve.** You buy one renewal and a worse loss next year.
- **Escalating without the facts.** A founder call with no data is a relationship call, and it burns the relationship.
- **No decision date.** A save plan without a date is a hope with a document attached.
- **Hiding the loss from the forecast.** The number is already wrong; the surprise is the part that costs trust.
