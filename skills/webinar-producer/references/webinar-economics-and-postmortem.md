# Webinar economics and postmortem

A webinar costs money, time and a piece of your list's attention. This file is how
to know whether it was worth it, in numbers a founder can act on.

## The cost stack

| Cost | Typical | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Platform | ₹0-6,000 per session | Meet is free; Zoom Webinar is per-seat per-month |
| Email tool | ₹0-2,500 | Usually already paid for |
| Paid promotion | ₹0-50,000 | The variable that decides the economics |
| Speaker time | 6-10 hours | Preparation is the real cost, not the hour |
| Producer time | 3-5 hours | Promotion, hosting, follow-up |
| Incentive or giveaway | ₹0-10,000 | Only if it attracts the right room |

Total for a small business, without paid promotion: ₹5,000-15,000 of cash and
about 12 person-hours. With paid promotion at ₹300 a registration and 150
registrations, add ₹45,000 - which is why the arithmetic in
`references/promotion-and-registration.md` comes before the ad budget.

## The four numbers

| Number | Where it comes from | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Invitations | Email tool + social reach | Effort, and the denominator for everything |
| Registrations | Registration report | Interest in the promise |
| Attended and stayed | Join/leave report at the offer minute | The real audience |
| Meetings booked within 14 days | CRM or the sheet | The only revenue-leading number |

Derived: cost per attendee (total cost ÷ attended), cost per meeting (total cost ÷
meetings), and revenue per meeting (closed revenue ÷ meetings, once known).

## Benchmarks to beat, not to quote

| Metric | Weak | Acceptable | Good |
|---|---|---|---|
| Registration → attendance | <25% | 30-40% | >45% |
| Stayed to the offer | <40% | 55-65% | >70% |
| Attended → meeting | <3% | 8-12% | >15% |
| Cost per meeting (paid promo) | >₹8,000 | ₹3,000-6,000 | <₹2,000 |

Use these only to judge your own trend. A B2B finance webinar and a skincare
masterclass do not share a benchmark.

## The postmortem, in 20 minutes

Fill this in the day after, before memory rewrites it:

1. **The promise** - as written on the landing page.
2. **The four numbers**, with the source of each.
3. **Where the room left** - the minute attendance dropped most, and what was
   happening then.
4. **The best question asked** - it is the next session's topic.
5. **What the follow-up produced** at day 14.
6. **One decision** - repeat this session, change the promise, change the
   audience, change the format, or stop.

## The decision rule

| If | Then |
|---|---|
| Meetings ≥ 8% of attended, cost per meeting under target | Repeat monthly with the same promise, new examples |
| Attendance strong, meetings weak | The offer or the audience is wrong - change one, not both |
| Registrations strong, attendance weak | Reminder ladder or timing - move the slot before rewriting the content |
| Everything weak, list small | Stop the webinar format; run a workshop for 12 named accounts instead |
| Cost per meeting above the cost of a cold call | Stop paid promotion; keep the session for the list |

## What a webinar is bad at

- **Reaching new audiences.** 80% of a typical room is people who already know you.
- **Closing.** It produces conversations, not signatures.
- **Frequency.** More than one a month fatigues the list and dilutes the promise.
- **Products that need a demo to be understood.** Use a workshop or a 1:1 instead.

## Anti-patterns

- **Reporting registrations as the result.** It flatters the campaign and hides
  the failure.
- **Judging a session before day 14.** Meetings arrive after the follow-up, not
  during the hour.
- **Changing three variables at once.** You learn nothing and repeat the mistake.
- **Quoting an industry benchmark as a target.** Your list is the benchmark.
- **Running the next session before the postmortem.** The same mistakes get a
  second audience.
