# Role scorecard

The scorecard is the hiring decision written down before anyone applies. It converts "we need a good operations manager" into five things that can be tested in an interview and checked in a year.

## The four parts

| Part | Question it answers | Example - warehouse operations manager |
|---|---|---|
| Mission | Why the role exists, in one sentence | Own dispatch accuracy and cost per shipment for two godowns |
| Outcomes | What must be true in 12 months | Dispatch accuracy above 99%, cost per shipment down 12%, two supervisors trained to run a shift alone |
| Competencies | What the person must be able to do | Shift planning, discrepancy investigation, vendor negotiation, team discipline |
| Behaviours | How they must work here | Escalates early, writes things down, does not shout at loaders |

## Writing an outcome that can be tested

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Improve warehouse efficiency" | "Reduce cost per shipment from ₹38 to ₹33 within four quarters, without a dispatch accuracy drop" |
| "Manage the team well" | "Two supervisors able to run a shift without the manager present by month 6" |
| "Handle vendors" | "Renegotiate three carrier contracts at renewal with a documented comparison" |
| "Be hands-on" | "Spend at least two shifts a week on the floor, evidenced by the shift log" |

Rule: an outcome has a **number, a date and a boundary** (what must not get worse).

## Competencies, split by how they are tested

| Type | Tested by | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Craft | A work sample or a case | "Here is a week of dispatch data with 14 discrepancies. Find the cause." |
| Judgement | A scenario question | "A carrier says the truck was loaded wrong; your loader says it was sealed correctly. What do you do in the next hour?" |
| Process discipline | A question about a past process they built | "Describe a checklist you introduced. What did it catch in the first month?" |
| Working with people | A reference, or a structured behavioural question | "Tell me about a time you had to correct a senior loader." |
| Motivation | A direct question | "What would make you leave this job in a year?" |

## Must-have versus nice-to-have

| Must-have | Nice-to-have |
|---|---|
| Cannot be learned in six months in this business | Teachable, or an accelerator |
| Fails the outcome without it | Improves speed or quality |
| Testable in the loop | Testable only over time |
| Written into the JD | Written as "adds an advantage" |

Cap must-haves at four. A JD with nine must-haves gets applicants who have none of them, because the people who have all nine are not applying to this role.

## The scorecard-to-JD handoff

| Scorecard element | Appears in the JD as |
|---|---|
| Mission | The first line under the role title |
| Outcomes | "In your first year you will" - three bullets, with numbers |
| Must-have competencies | "What you must have done before" |
| Nice-to-have | "Adds an advantage" |
| Behaviours | "How we work" - stated as practice, not as values words |
| Hard parts | "What is hard about this job" - the section that saves six months |

## Sign-off before sourcing

The hiring manager and the person paying the salary both sign the scorecard. Without that, the first shortlist gets argued about by criteria nobody agreed to.

## Anti-patterns

- **Writing the JD first and reverse-engineering a scorecard.** The JD becomes a list of what the last person had.
- **Ten must-haves.** It filters for nothing and signals a team that has not decided what the role is.
- **Outcomes with no number.** "Improve" cannot be tested, so it becomes a performance argument in month nine.
- **Competencies that are personality adjectives.** "Energetic" and "passionate" test nothing and exclude quietly.
- **Skipping the hard parts.** The best candidates ask about them anyway; hiding them wastes both sides' time.
- **Changing the scorecard after seeing the shortlist.** It converts a process into a rationalisation.
