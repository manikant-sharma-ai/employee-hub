# Creative brief and feedback

Most design that fails was never briefed. It was requested. This file is how you turn a request into a brief in one round, and how you survive the feedback that follows.

## The brief, in six lines

Write it back to them before you design. Six lines, no more.

```
Job:        <the one thing the viewer should do or feel>
Placement:  <surface, size, print or screen>
Audience:   <who, and what they already know about us>
Message:    <the single sentence, in their words>
Proof:      <the number, the demo, the before/after that makes it believable>
Must/never: <mandatory text, banned colours, things the boss hates>
```

If line 4 and line 5 are the same thing, the message has no proof and the asset will be pretty and unconvincing.

## Diagnosing a vague request

| What they said | What they usually mean | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| "Make it pop" | It has no focal point | "What should someone notice first?" |
| "Make it premium" | Too many elements, cheap type | "Which of these three references feels right?" |
| "Make it modern" | It looks dated | "Dated how - the type, the colours, or the layout?" |
| "Can you just..." | They think it is five minutes | "Yes - here is what it needs to be correct." |
| "Same as last time but different" | They want a variant, not a new direction | "Which parts stay?" |
| "The client wants it bigger" | The client cannot read it | "Bigger, or clearer? Those are different fixes." |

Ask one question, not five. Five questions reads as unwillingness.

## Presenting work

- **Two options, and a recommendation.** Option A is your recommendation. Option B is a genuinely different direction, not a variation of A. Six options means you have not decided, and it pushes the decision back onto them.
- **Present in context.** A post shown on a mock phone at real size, a flyer shown at A5, a thumbnail shown at 168 px next to two competitors. Context does the arguing.
- **Lead with the job, not the design.** "This reads as a ₹499 offer at a glance, which is what you asked for." Then the craft notes.
- **Say what you would change if you had another round.** It signals you are not finished because you ran out of ideas, and it makes the current version look considered rather than final.

## Taking feedback

The rule: **feedback about the job is actionable, feedback about taste is negotiable, and both are legitimate.**

1. **Separate the two.** "I don't like orange" is taste. "I can't tell what the price is" is the job.
2. **Fix the job first**, in front of them if possible. It is usually one change, and it often resolves the taste complaint too.
3. **Ask for the reason, once.** "What is it doing that does not work for you?" A reason turns taste into a brief. No reason after one ask means you accept it or you push back with the job as your argument.
4. **Push back with the job, never with your feelings.** "I can make the logo bigger, but at this size it competes with the price and the price is what sells this" is a real argument. "I think it looks better this way" is not.
5. **Cap the rounds.** Two rounds included, a third round is a new brief. Say this at the start; it is not rude, it is a schedule.
6. **When two stakeholders disagree**, do not average them into mush. Ask who owns the outcome, and design for that person.

## Version discipline

- Every round gets a `vN`, a date, and one line on what changed. Feedback gets lost otherwise, and you will be asked to undo a change you already made.
- Keep the rejected version. "Can we go back to the first one?" is asked more often than anyone expects.
- If a change is requested that contradicts an earlier decision, cite the decision and the date. This is what `notes/decisions.md` is for.

## Anti-patterns

- Designing before the copy is final, then reflowing everything. The most common wasted round in the business.
- Accepting "make it pop" and delivering a gradient. It does not answer the question and it invites another round.
- Presenting work you do not recommend, to avoid an argument. They will pick it.
- Defending a choice after the person who owns the outcome has decided. Note your objection once, in `notes/decisions.md`, and build it.
- Redesigning the whole asset to fix one line of text. Targeted fixes read as craft; wholesale changes read as indecision.
- No brief, no version numbers, no decisions log - then three months later nobody can say why the logo is on the left.
