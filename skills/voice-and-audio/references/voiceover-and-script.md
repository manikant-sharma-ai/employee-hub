# Voiceover and script

A voiceover script is not prose read aloud. It is a set of instructions to a
mouth, a breath and a clock.

## Writing to the clock

| Language and pace | Words per minute | 15 s | 30 s | 60 s | 90 s |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| English, measured narration | 130-140 | 33-35 | 65-70 | 130-140 | 195-210 |
| English, energetic ad read | 150-160 | 38-40 | 75-80 | 150-160 | 225-240 |
| Hindi, measured | 110-125 | 28-31 | 55-62 | 110-125 | 165-190 |
| Hinglish, conversational | 125-140 | 31-35 | 62-70 | 125-140 | 190-210 |
| Tamil or Bengali, measured | 105-120 | 26-30 | 53-60 | 105-120 | 158-180 |

Write 10% under the target. A read that lands at 29.5 seconds is a good 30; one
that lands at 31 is a re-record.

## Script format

```
[00:00] (warm, low) Diwali is when the whole street lights up.
        (beat)
[00:04] But your electricity bill arrives in the same week.
        (beat, music in)
[00:08] This year, the lights cost 40% less.
[00:11] (up) Same brightness. Same street. Half the bill.
[00:15] (tag, flat) <Brand>. Switch on, spend less.
```

Rules: one idea per line, a breath mark wherever a human would breathe, delivery
notes in brackets and in lowercase, numbers written the way they are said
("forty percent", not "40%"), and every acronym spelled out phonetically the
first time.

## Pronunciation guide - always

For every Indian name, place, brand and number in the script:

| Written | Say it | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Srinivas Iyengar | shree-ni-VAAS eye-YEN-gar | stress on the second syllable |
| Chandrayaan | chun-druh-YAAN | not "chand-ra-yan" |
| ₹4,50,000 | four lakh fifty thousand | never "four point five lakh" |
| GST | jee-ess-tee | not "gist" |
| Ananthagiri | uh-nun-thuh-GI-ri | soft th |
| 2026 | twenty twenty-six | unless the brand says "two thousand" |

Ask the person to send a 10-second voice note of the hardest name. It costs them
a minute and saves a re-record.

## Hinglish and code-switching

- **Switch at the noun, not mid-clause.** "Aaj ka offer - 40% off on all kurtas" reads naturally; a switch every four words reads like a translation.
- **Keep the call to action in one language.** A CTA split across two languages loses the verb.
- **Write the Devanagari line beside the Roman one** for the reader who is more comfortable in one script.
- **Numbers in the listener's frame.** "Chaar sau rupaye" for a mass-market audience, "four hundred rupees" for a premium one.

## Direction notes that change a read

| Note | What it does |
|---|---|
| (smile) | Lifts the first two formants; the listener hears warmth |
| (down) | Drops the last three words; makes a statement final |
| (beat) | A half-second pause; the cheapest emphasis there is |
| (flat, tag) | Removes the sing-song; the brand line should not perform |
| (conspiratorial) | Closer mic, lower volume - works for a hook, fails for a price |

## Anti-patterns

- **Writing the script before knowing the slot length.** The length decides the argument, not the other way round.
- **Three adjectives in a row.** The reader has one breath and one emphasis; three adjectives get none.
- **A question the listener cannot answer.** "Tired of the same old?" invites a no.
- **Numbers at the end of a sentence** where the read speeds up and the number blurs.
- **No alternate take requested.** Ask for two reads of the CTA and the hook; the choice is made in the edit, not in the studio.
