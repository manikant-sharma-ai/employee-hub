# Message craft and channel

The message is short, specific, and asks for one thing. This file writes it, per stage and per channel.

## The anatomy of a follow-up that gets a reply

| Part | Length | Job |
|---|---|---|
| The reference | 1 sentence | Proves a human was listening |
| The new thing | 1-2 sentences | Gives a reason to reply now |
| The ask | 1 question | Makes replying easy |
| The exit | Half a sentence | Gives permission to say no, which raises the reply rate |

Four to six lines in total on email. Under 60 words on WhatsApp. A follow-up longer than the original proposal
will not be read.

## By stage

**After a call, before a quote**

> "You said the 40-seat requirement is fixed but the reporting module is optional. I have priced both - the
> optional module adds ₹18,000 a year. Should I send the version without it first, so your partner sees the
> smaller number?"

**After a quote, no response (touch 1)**

> "The quote I sent on 4 November holds the ₹1,240 rate until 30 November. One thing I did not ask: is the
> installation at one site or two? It changes the freight line by about ₹6,000."

**After a quote, no response (touch 2)**

> "I am assuming this has gone to your accounts team. If it is easier, I can send a one-page summary with the
> three numbers your MD will ask about - total cost, delivery date, and what happens if the volume doubles."

**After a demo**

> "You asked whether it works on a phone with poor network. It does - offline mode caches the last 24 hours.
> Should I set up a trial on your own data so your supervisor can test it in the field this week?"

**Trial running**

> "Day 4 of the trial. Two of your supervisors have logged in, one has not - is there a shift timing I should
> work around for the training?"

**Decision pending on their side**

> "You said the board meets on the 22nd. I will not write again before then. If it helps, here is a half-page
> with the three questions boards usually ask about this."

**The last touch, before the stop rule**

> "I have not heard back, so I will assume the timing is not right and stop here. If it becomes a priority later,
> the pricing I quoted is valid for six months from today. Should I close this for now?"

That last message recovers a meaningful share of dead deals, because it asks for a decision rather than attention.

## Channel choice

| Channel | Use when | Length | Do not |
|---|---|---|---|
| Email | The conversation started there, or a document is involved | 60-120 words | Attach a 12-page deck to a nudge |
| WhatsApp | The buyer wrote there first, or the trade runs on it | Under 60 words, voice note if that is how they talk | Broadcast lists, groups, forwarded chains |
| Phone call | Touch 4, or a decision date has passed | 2 minutes of their time | Call without knowing the last exchange |
| LinkedIn or social DM | B2B where the buyer is active there and email failed | 40 words | Pitch in the first message |
| In person | Field sales, where the beat already takes someone there | - | Turn a courtesy visit into a pitch |

The rule: **the channel of the conversation, then one escalation, never more.** A buyer who has ignored three
emails will not answer the fourth, and will remember the WhatsApp message that followed it.

## Language and register

- **Reply in the language the buyer wrote in.** A Hindi enquiry answered in formal English loses warmth; a Tamil
  WhatsApp answered in English loses the thread.
- **Match the register, not just the language.** "Sir" and "kindly revert" is right for some buyers and wrong for
  a 28-year-old founder. Copy the buyer's own greeting.
- **Write for a forwarded email.** Assume the message will be read by someone who was not in the conversation -
  name the deal, the quantity and the price so it survives forwarding.
- **No emojis in a first follow-up to a new buyer**; match whatever they used afterwards.
- **Never use "Dear Sir/Madam" in a follow-up.** You have spoken to this person; you know their name.

## The asks that get answered

| Ask | Answer rate | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A yes/no on a specific point | Highest | One keystroke |
| A choice between two options | High | Feels like progress |
| A date confirmation | High | Low effort, concrete |
| "What are your thoughts?" | Low | Invites an essay |
| "Please revert at the earliest" | Lowest | Puts the work on them, with no specific thing to do |
| Three questions in one email | Low | One gets answered, usually the easiest |

## What the follow-up must never do

- **Add a new requirement or a new document request** on touch 3 that was not mentioned on touch 1. It resets the
  buyer's evaluation and reads as disorganisation.
- **Escalate emotionally** - "I have sent three emails and received no response" is a complaint, not a follow-up.
- **Offer a discount to get a reply.** It teaches the buyer that silence produces discounts, and it will be
  repeated on the next deal.
- **Use false urgency.** A price validity date is real; "this offer expires tonight" for a product with no
  scarcity is a lie the buyer checks.
- **Include a calendar link on every touch.** A booking link on touch 1 is efficient; on touch 4 it is a refusal
  to engage with what the buyer said.

## Anti-patterns

- The wall of text that restates the entire proposal because the writer did not check what was sent.
- A follow-up that says "as discussed" when nothing was discussed.
- Sending the same template to six buyers in the same company, who compare.
- Attaching the same PDF on every touch, so the buyer stops opening it.
- A message that ends with two questions, one of which is "let me know".
- Signing off with a marketing banner and three social links in a one-to-one message.
