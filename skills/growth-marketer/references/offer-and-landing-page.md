# Offer and landing page

Most traffic problems are offer problems, and most conversion problems are clarity
problems. Fix these before buying another click.

## The offer, before the page

An offer is four things, and a weak one cannot be rescued by design:

1. **What it is**, in the buyer's words, not the category's.
2. **Who it is for**, specifically enough that someone can rule themselves out.
3. **What it costs**, or the range, or why the price is not shown.
4. **What happens next**, and how fast.

If any of the four is missing, the page is doing the work of a salesperson who
never got briefed.

## The first screen

One screen, five questions answered without scrolling:

| Question | Where it is answered |
|---|---|
| What is this? | The headline, in plain words |
| Who is it for? | A sub-headline naming the buyer |
| Why should I believe it? | One proof: a number, a name, a demonstration |
| What does it cost? | The price or the starting price, or "get a quote in 24 hours" |
| What do I do now? | One button, one action |

Two buttons of equal weight is a decision the visitor did not ask to make.

## The five objections

Every buyer has them; the page either answers them or loses the sale silently.

| Objection | What answers it |
|---|---|
| "Is this for someone like me?" | A named customer type and a case that resembles them |
| "Will it work?" | Evidence: a result with a number, a demo, a sample, a guarantee |
| "Is it worth the price?" | Comparison to the cost of the problem, not to a competitor's price |
| "Can I trust you?" | Real address, real phone, GST details, return policy, reviews with names |
| "What if it goes wrong?" | The return, refund, warranty or support promise in one line |

The trust block matters more in India than in most markets: a phone number that
answers, a landline-era address and a clear return policy move conversion more than
a redesign.

## Form length

| Fields | Effect |
|---|---|
| 2-3 | Highest completion, lowest qualification |
| 4-5 | The usual balance for a considered purchase |
| 6+ | Only when the sale needs the detail; completion drops sharply |

Every extra field is a filter. Decide whether you are filtering for volume or
quality, and measure qualified leads, not leads.

## Page speed and mobile reality

- **Most Indian consumer traffic is mobile, often on a mid-range Android on a
  congested network.** A 4-second page is a 40% loss before the offer is read.
- **Images are the weight.** Compress, lazy-load, and check on a real device rather
  than a laptop with a fast connection.
- **Payment and address entry are the leak.** UPI first, saved addresses, and a
  pin-code check before the address form.
- **Language.** A page in the buyer's language often outperforms a better page in
  English; test it rather than assuming.

## Copy rules that survive contact with a buyer

- **Lead with the outcome, not the feature.** "Stock that reconciles to your
  gateway" beats "AI-powered inventory sync".
- **Numbers beat adjectives.** "Ships in 48 hours to 12,000 pin codes" beats "fast
  delivery".
- **One idea per section**, and no more than three sections before the call to action.
- **Say the price.** Hidden pricing loses the buyer who is comparison shopping,
  which is most of them.
- **No invented urgency.** "Only 2 left" when there are 200 is a trust loss that
  costs more than it earns.

## Anti-patterns

- **The brand film above the fold.** It delays the answer to "what is this".
- **Testimonials with no name, company or result.** They read as decoration.
- **A page built for the founder's peers** rather than for the buyer.
- **Chat widget over the call to action.** It competes with the one action you want.
- **The redesign before the offer.** A new page for a weak offer converts a little
  better and costs a lot more.
- **Discount as the first lever.** It trains the buyer to wait, and it caps the
  price permanently; the pricing owner decides discounts, not the page.
