# Margin and unit economics

The floor is arithmetic. This file is the arithmetic, with the cost lines small
businesses forget.

## Contribution margin, built honestly

| Line | Per unit / per customer-month | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Price realised (after discount) | | Not the list price |
| − Material or licence cost | | Landed, including freight and duty |
| − Payment gateway fee | | 1.5-2.5% typical; check the actual rate |
| − Delivery or fulfilment | | Including returns and re-shipment |
| − Support cost | | Minutes × loaded hourly cost ÷ customers |
| − Onboarding cost | | Hours ÷ expected lifetime in months |
| − Hosting or infrastructure | | Per customer, not total |
| − Sales cost, if attributable | | Commission, or CAC amortised over lifetime |
| **= Contribution margin** | | The number the floor is set from |

Contribution margin as a percentage of realised price is the number to quote.
Gross margin from the accounting system usually excludes several of these lines and
is optimistic by 5-15 points.

## Cost to serve, measured

- **Support minutes per customer per month.** Read from the helpdesk, not estimated.
  A customer on WhatsApp costs 3-5× one on email.
- **Onboarding hours.** The real number, including the second call nobody logs.
- **Returns and refunds** as a percentage of revenue, by channel.
- **Failed payment and dunning cost** - gateway fees on retries, and the hours spent.

## The floor

> **Floor = variable cost per unit ÷ (1 − minimum acceptable contribution margin)**

Worked: variable cost ₹700, minimum contribution margin 30% → floor = ₹700 ÷ 0.7 =
₹1,000. Below that, each sale consumes cash the business needs for the next one.

Set the floor per channel: a reseller at 30% off list must still clear the floor, or
the channel is buying revenue with your money.

## Price change sensitivity

The two numbers to compute for any change:

1. **Break-even volume.** How much volume can you lose before profit falls?
2. **Profit at three volumes.** -10%, flat, +10%.

Worked: price ₹1,000, variable cost ₹600, contribution ₹400, 1,000 units → ₹4,00,000.
Raise price 10% to ₹1,100: contribution ₹500. Break-even units = 4,00,000 ÷ 500 = 800.
So you can lose **20% of volume** and hold the same profit. Most businesses overestimate
how much volume a 10% rise costs.

| Change | Contribution per unit | Break-even units | Volume you can lose |
|---|---|---|---|
| −10% price | ₹300 | 1,333 | Cannot - you need +33% volume |
| Flat | ₹400 | 1,000 | 0% |
| +10% price | ₹500 | 800 | 20% |
| +20% price | ₹600 | 667 | 33% |

That table is the single most useful slide in a pricing conversation: **cutting price
requires a volume increase that is almost never available.**

## The 1% rule

On a 10% net margin, a 1% price increase with no volume loss adds about 10% to
operating profit. On a 5% margin, it adds about 20%. Cost-cutting to find the same
profit usually means people, and pricing does not.

## Lifetime and payback

| Metric | Formula | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| CAC | Sales and marketing spend ÷ new customers | Including salaries, not just ad spend |
| Payback months | CAC ÷ monthly contribution margin | Over 12 months needs a reason |
| LTV | Monthly contribution ÷ monthly churn rate | Churn measured, not assumed |
| LTV:CAC | Ratio | 3:1 is a healthy rule of thumb, not a law |

## Discounts and the margin they eat

A 10% discount on a 40% contribution margin removes 25% of the contribution. A 20%
discount removes half. Show that arithmetic to the person approving the discount -
it changes the conversation faster than any policy.

## Anti-patterns

- Gross margin from the books presented as contribution margin.
- Gateway fees and delivery left out because they are "small".
- Support cost estimated at zero because it is not invoiced.
- A floor set from the average cost, when the marginal customer costs more.
- A CAC that counts ad spend and not the salesperson.
- A price rise modelled with volume loss assumed at zero.
