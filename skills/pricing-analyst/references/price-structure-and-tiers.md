# Price structure and tiers

Structure decides more revenue than the number on the page. A good structure makes the right choice obvious; a bad one makes every buyer ask a question.

## The three shapes

| Shape | When it works | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Single price | One product, one buyer type, low consideration | Leaves money on the table at the top end |
| Good-better-best (3 tiers) | Different levels of need exist and are visible | A middle tier nobody chooses, or a top tier nobody can justify |
| Usage / metered | Cost to serve scales with usage | Unpredictable bills that scare buyers away |

## Good-better-best, done properly

| Tier | Job | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | Remove the price objection, not the value | Must be genuinely usable, or it teaches the buyer you are not serious |
| Middle | The one you want sold | Should be the obvious answer for the target segment - 60-70% of buyers |
| Top | Anchor, and serve the top 10% | Must be buyable and deliverable, or it is a lie that damages trust |

Design the middle tier first. Then build the entry tier by removing the two features the target segment will not miss, and the top tier by adding the two things the top 10% actually ask for.

**The decoy rule.** A decoy is a tier priced to make another look good. It works only if it is a real, deliverable offer. A tier that cannot be bought is a trick buyers notice, and it costs more trust than it earns.

## Anchoring and the order of the page

- **Show the top tier first** on the page, left to right or top to bottom - the first number sets the frame.
- **Annual before monthly**, with the saving in rupees, not percent.
- **One recommended badge**, not three.
- **The comparison table must be readable in ten seconds** - if a buyer needs a spreadsheet, they leave.

## Pricing axes that fit Indian buying

| Axis | Good for | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| Per seat / per user | Teams, SaaS | Punishes sharing; invites seat-sharing and password sharing |
| Per location / per outlet | Retail, restaurants, clinics | Natural for multi-outlet businesses |
| Per transaction | Payments, logistics, marketplaces | Aligns cost to serve; unpredictable for the buyer |
| Per volume band | Manufacturing, wholesale | Clear, and easy to compare against a competitor |
| Flat monthly | Small businesses, agencies | Simple to sell, hard to grow revenue |
| Annual prepay | Cash flow | A financing decision - price it as one |

## The annual prepay discount

If a monthly plan is ₹1,000, a year is ₹12,000. A "two months free" annual price of ₹10,000 is a 16.7% discount, not a small courtesy. Its real cost is the cash you did not collect for eleven months, and it is worth it only if churn or cash flow justifies it. Compute the effective annualised cost before offering it.

## Rounding and price points

- **₹X99 works** in consumer categories where the price is compared at a glance.
- **Round numbers work** in B2B and procurement, where a ₹1,499 quote reads as a consumer trick.
- **Never price below a competitor by ₹1.** It signals that the only difference is the price.
- **Avoid ₹1,497.30.** A price with paise reads as a calculation, not a decision.
- **Price per unit of the thing people compare** - per litre, per kg, per wash - when pack sizes differ.

## When to restructure rather than reprice

Restructure when: the same price produces different value for different segments; when support cost is concentrated in one segment; when a customer's usage varies 20x; when the entry tier is cannibalising the middle.

Reprice when: costs have moved, the segment's willingness has moved, or the current price was set by copying and never revisited.

Do not do both in the same month if you want to know which one worked.

## Anti-patterns

- Three tiers where the middle is a slightly smaller version of the top and the entry is unusable.
- A "contact us" top tier that is really "we have not priced it".
- Feature-gating something the buyer needs to evaluate the product.
- A per-seat price that punishes the team for adopting it.
- Discounting the annual plan without computing the financing cost.
- Changing structure and price at once, then arguing about which caused the churn.
