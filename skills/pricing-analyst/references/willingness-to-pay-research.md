# Willingness to pay

What someone will pay is a fact about their alternatives, not about your product. Research exists to find the alternatives, not to ask people to guess a number.

## Ranked evidence

| Rank | Evidence | Strength | What it can support |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Actual sales at two or more price points | Strongest | A band, and a direction |
| 2 | A priced pilot or pre-order with real money | Strong | Whether the price clears at all |
| 3 | Win-loss interviews where price was the reason | Moderate | The objection, and its size |
| 4 | Van Westendorp or Gabor-Granger on 100+ responses | Moderate | A band, not a point |
| 5 | A survey of 30-50 customers | Weak | Direction, order of magnitude |
| 6 | A competitor's public price | Weak | A reference point, nothing more |
| 7 | The founder's hunch | Weakest | A hypothesis to test |

## The four questions (Van Westendorp)

Ask about the *category*, not your product:

1. At what price would this be so cheap you would doubt the quality?
2. At what price is it a bargain - good value?
3. At what price does it start to feel expensive, but you would still consider it?
4. At what price is it too expensive to consider at all?

The overlaps give an acceptable range and an indifference price. Useful for a band. Dangerous as a point estimate, and worse with fewer than 100 responses.

## Gabor-Granger, when there is a real list

Show the same offer at different prices, to different people, and record purchase intent at each. Fit intent against price. Needs 100+ per price point to say anything, which is why most small businesses should skip it and run a priced test instead.

## The questions that actually work

Ask about the past and the alternative, never the future:

- "What did you use before this, and what did it cost?"
- "What do you do today when this problem comes up?"
- "When was the last time you paid for something like this - how much was it?"
- "What would you have to stop paying for to afford this?"
- "Who else is in the decision, and what do they compare it to?"
- "At what price would you have said no?"

The last one is the most useful. It produces a real number and the reason behind it.

## Indian market realities

- **B2B opens with a discount expectation.** The first price is a starting position, and the discount ladder (see `discount-and-negotiation-policy.md`) is part of the pricing design, not a failure of it.
- **Anchoring on the monthly number.** ₹999/month is read very differently from ₹11,988/year, even though they are the same. Show both when the commitment is annual.
- **Price signals quality in categories where trust is low** - supplements, coaching, healthcare, B2B services. Pricing below the category floor can reduce demand.
- **"Per month" versus "per user per month"** changes the comparison set entirely. A per-user price invites comparison with a cheaper per-user tool.
- **GST changes the number the buyer actually pays.** For B2B, quote exclusive and show the GST line; for B2C, quote inclusive and never surprise at checkout.
- **Marketplace prices are visible and compared.** A D2C price above the Amazon price for the same SKU needs an explanation the customer can see.

## Reading the results honestly

| Sample | What you may say |
|---|---|
| 10-30 responses | "Directional. Three of five said they would pay above ₹1,000." |
| 50-100 | "The band is roughly ₹900-1,400, wide." |
| 100+ with a priced test | "The band is ₹1,100-1,300 and conversion held at ₹1,199." |
| A/B test in production | "Revenue per visitor was higher at ₹1,199 over three weeks." |

Never turn the first row into the fourth.

## Anti-patterns

- "What would you pay for this?" - invites a lowball and teaches the respondent to negotiate.
- Surveying only existing customers, who have already accepted the current price.
- Treating intent as purchase.
- Using a competitor's price as the market price.
- Researching for three weeks when a priced test for two weeks would answer it.
- Presenting a range with no sample size next to it.
