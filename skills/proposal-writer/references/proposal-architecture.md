# Proposal architecture

The evaluator reads with a scoring sheet and a clock. Structure the document so that
finding an answer takes seconds.

## The standard structure

| Section | Length share | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Cover and index | 1 page | Identify the bid, the reference number, the date |
| Executive summary | 1 page | The three things that win, in the client's language |
| Understanding of the requirement | 1-2 pages | Prove the document was read, in their words |
| Proposed solution and methodology | 30-40% | How the requirement is met, phase by phase |
| Team and organisation | 10% | Who does it, with CVs and roles |
| Relevant experience | 10-15% | Three to five projects, each with a number |
| Implementation plan and timeline | 5-10% | Dates, dependencies, milestones |
| Quality, risk and governance | 5-10% | How it is controlled, and what happens when it is not |
| Commercial response | Per the client's format | Price, options, assumptions |
| Compliance matrix and deviations | Per the client's format | The proof of completeness |
| Annexures | As required | Forms, certificates, references, EMD |

If the client prescribes a structure, use theirs exactly. Their numbering, their headings,
their order - even when it is clumsy. A compliant document that is hard to navigate beats
an elegant one that skips a section.

## The executive summary, written last

One page, four paragraphs, no adjectives:

1. **What we understand** - the client's problem in one sentence, in their words.
2. **What we propose** - the solution in two sentences, with the differentiator.
3. **Why us** - the two proof points that match the highest-scoring criteria.
4. **The commercial headline** - the price or the price range, and the validity.

The summary is the only page every evaluator reads. It is not an introduction; it is the
whole argument in one page.

## Answering a requirement

Every scored requirement gets the same four-part answer:

| Part | Content | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Restate | The requirement in the client's words | "Requirement 4.2: 24×7 support with a 2-hour response." |
| Respond | The commitment, unambiguously | "We provide 24×7 support with a 2-hour response for P1, 8 hours for P2." |
| Evidence | The proof | "SLA extract, Annexure D; current average response 47 minutes across 14 clients." |
| Reference | Where the detail is | "Escalation matrix, section 4.3." |

Four parts, four lines. A section that answers nine requirements in nine such blocks scores
better than nine pages of narrative that answers them implicitly.

## Page budget

| Total limit | Summary | Solution | Experience | Team | Commercial |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 pages | 1 | 4 | 2 | 1 | 2 |
| 25 pages | 1 | 10 | 5 | 3 | 4 |
| 50 pages | 1-2 | 20 | 8 | 6 | 8 |
| No limit | Still budget | 40% | 15% | 10% | 20% |

"No page limit" does not mean "no length discipline". A 120-page proposal is read by
nobody, and the sections that score are buried.

## Writing for an evaluator

- **Use their nouns.** If they say "consignment", do not say "shipment".
- **Number everything** to match the matrix. Section 3.2 answers requirement 3.2.
- **Put the answer first**, the explanation second. An evaluator scanning for a commitment
  should find it in the first line.
- **Tables over paragraphs** for specifications, SLAs, timelines and compliance.
- **No company history in the solution section.** It belongs in the experience section,
  once, briefly.
- **No "we" without a proof point** within two sentences.

## What to cut

| Cut | Because |
|---|---|
| The company's founding story | It scores nothing |
| Generic methodology diagrams with no client content | They read as boilerplate |
| Every client ever served | Three relevant ones beat thirty irrelevant |
| Marketing language | "World-class", "cutting-edge", "seamless" - all noise |
| A section that answers no matrix row | It costs pages and attention |
| The same paragraph twice, in two sections | It signals a template, not a response |

## The evidence library

Keep it ready, not assembled per bid:

- Six to ten project summaries, each with scope, value, dates, outcome and a metric.
- CVs, two pages each, tailored per bid with a one-line relevance statement.
- Certificates with expiry dates, in date order.
- Financial statements for the last three years, plus the turnover certificate.
- Three client references with permission to contact.
- Standard policy documents: quality, safety, data protection, code of conduct.

A bid assembled from a good library takes two days. One assembled from scratch takes two
weeks and loses the deadline.

## Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Cost |
|---|---|
| Company template imposed on a prescribed structure | Marks lost for non-compliance |
| Executive summary written first | It becomes an introduction instead of an argument |
| Narrative answers to tabular requirements | The evaluator cannot find the commitment |
| 90 pages with no page limit | Nobody reads past page 12 |
| Same project summary in every bid | Reads as boilerplate, and the relevance is not argued |
| No evidence for a claim | Scored as absent |
