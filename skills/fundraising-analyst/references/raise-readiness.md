# Raise readiness

Most raises fail before the first meeting, in the founder's own spreadsheet. This
is the checklist that decides whether to start, and the honest reasons to wait.

## The four questions, answered in order

1. **How many months of cash?** Under 6, the answer is a bridge or revenue, not a priced round - a priced round takes 4-6 months in India and longer at seed.
2. **What is the milestone the next round needs?** A seed round buys the Series A story: usually ₹8-15 crore ARR with proven retention, or a category-defining usage curve.
3. **Does the current data support a story an investor already funds?** A fund with a thesis about B2B SaaS will not stretch to a services business with software attached.
4. **Is there a warm path to 15 investors?** Cold inbound converts at a few percent; a warm intro at 20-40%.

## Readiness by stage

| Stage | Typical raise | What must be true | What kills it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-seed | ₹50 lakh-2 crore | Founder insight, early signal, a plan | No product, no signal, no clarity on use of funds |
| Seed | ₹3-12 crore | ₹40 lakh-4 crore ARR or strong usage, retention evidence | CAC unknown, churn hidden, cap table messy |
| Series A | ₹25-80 crore | ₹8-15 crore ARR, 80%+ gross margin for SaaS, NRR above 100% | Growth bought with discounts, no repeatable channel |
| Series B | ₹80-250 crore | Unit economics proven at scale, a second product or geography | Growth slowing with no margin improvement |

These are bands, not rules. A fund's own cheque size and ownership target decide
the round size more than the plan does - a ₹100 crore fund writing ₹8 crore needs
10-15% ownership, which sets the valuation conversation.

## The ten things diligence finds, in order of frequency

1. Cap table not updated since the last round, or ESOP promised verbally.
2. Founder agreements without IP assignment.
3. Statutory dues - TDS, GST, PF - unpaid or unfiled, discovered in the bank statements.
4. Revenue recognised on bookings rather than delivery.
5. Related-party transactions with a founder's other company.
6. Customer concentration nobody mentioned - one client at 40% of revenue.
7. Contracts with no termination or assignment clause.
8. Data handling with no privacy notice, and personal data in spreadsheets.
9. A co-founder who left with equity and no vesting.
10. A loan or a convertible note with an undisclosed side letter.

Fix the top five before the first meeting. Each one is cheap now and expensive in
a diligence call.

## The two-month pre-raise sprint

| Week | Work |
|---|---|
| 1-2 | Cap table, IP assignments, statutory dues cleaned. Books closed to the last month. |
| 3-4 | Model built from actuals, three cases, cash check. Metric definitions agreed. |
| 5-6 | Data room assembled and indexed. Red flags written with answers. |
| 7-8 | Target list of 25-40, warm paths mapped, intro requests out. |

Do not start outreach before week 7. A first meeting with a fund you will want in
six months is a wasted first meeting.

## When not to raise

- **When the metric that justifies the round is one month old.** One good month is a data point, not a trend.
- **When the round would be raised at a lower valuation than the last one.** A down round resets every option pool and every expectation; a bridge or revenue is often better.
- **When the founder cannot say what the money buys in hires and months.** Investors hear that as "we will figure it out".
- **When the only reason is that a competitor raised.** Their round is not your plan.

## Anti-patterns

- **Raising because the runway is short.** The negotiating position is worst exactly when the need is greatest.
- **A model built in the deck rather than in a spreadsheet.** It will not foot when questioned.
- **Waiting for perfect metrics.** There is a threshold, not a perfect; below it, wait, above it, go.
- **Twenty simultaneous processes.** It signals desperation and everyone hears about it.
