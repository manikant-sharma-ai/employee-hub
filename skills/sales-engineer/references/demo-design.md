# Demo design

A demo is not a product tour. It is a short argument, made with the buyer's own workflow,
that the thing they are worried about will work.

## The five-scene structure

| Scene | Purpose | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Their world | restate the problem in their words, with their numbers | 2 min |
| 2. The moment of proof | the one capability that answers their hardest question | 4 min |
| 3. The daily workflow | their Tuesday, end to end, in the product | 8 min |
| 4. The integration | the deciding system, connected, with real data | 5 min |
| 5. The edge they asked about | the scale, the exception, the approval, the export | 5 min |

Then stop. A 25-minute demo with five minutes of questions beats a 50-minute tour with
none.

## Scene 2 is the whole demo

The moment of proof is the answer to the hardest question the buyer asked in discovery -
not the flashiest feature. If they said "our problem is reconciling 40,000 invoices", scene
two is 40,000 invoices reconciling, not the dashboard.

Prepare it so it cannot fail: pre-loaded data, a known-good path, and a screenshot of the
result in the deck in case the environment misbehaves.

## Data

- **Their data** is best, with written permission and a retention rule.
- **A realistic stand-in** is second - same field names, same volumes, same edge cases.
- **Lorem ipsum or "Acme Corp"** is a signal that nobody prepared.

Say which one it is, out loud, in scene one. A buyer who thinks the numbers are theirs and
finds out later that they are not has learned something about you, not the product.

## The failure plan

Every demo has one scene that breaks. Prepare for it:

- **Know which scene it is** and have a screenshot or a recording of it working.
- **Say what you are doing** - "this environment is shared; I'll show you the recorded run
  from this morning" is credible. Silence while clicking is not.
- **Never debug live** for more than 30 seconds. Move on, come back at the end.
- **Never blame the environment** in a way that implies it happens often.

## The script

One page, left column the scene, right column the exact clicks and the sentence that
introduces it. Write the sentence - the words matter more than the clicks.

Include: what you will skip, and why. "I'm not going to show you reporting, because you
told me reporting is not the problem - I'll send the recording." That single line buys
more credibility than any feature.

## Who is in the room

- **The technical stakeholder** - the demo is for them. Everything else is audience.
- **The economic buyer** - wants the outcome and the risk, not the clicks; give them one
  sentence per scene.
- **The champion** - wants to look good; give them the moment of proof to repeat later.
- **The blocker** - usually IT or security; find their question and answer it in scene 4
  or 5 before they ask.

## Anti-patterns

- **The feature tour** organised by menu, which is how the product is built rather than
  how the buyer works.
- **Starting with the company history.**
- **Showing the admin settings** to a business buyer, or the business case to an engineer.
- **A demo with no questions in it.** If nobody interrupts, nobody is imagining using it.
- **The surprise roadmap slide** at the end, which turns a technical win into a
  negotiation about what does not exist.
