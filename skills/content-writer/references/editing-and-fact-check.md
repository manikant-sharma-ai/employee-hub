# Editing and fact-check

Drafting is half the job. The other half is what makes a piece publishable: cutting it to its load-bearing sentences and checking that every claim survives being questioned.

## Three passes, in this order

Never do all three at once. They conflict.

| Pass | Question | What you change |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural** | Is the argument in the right order? | Move, merge, delete whole sections |
| **Line** | Is each sentence doing work? | Cut, tighten, split, reorder within a paragraph |
| **Copy** | Is it correct and consistent? | Spelling, numbers, names, units, dates, style |

Editing copy before structure wastes the work: you polish sentences you are about to delete.

## The structural pass

- Read only the headings. Do they make an argument? If not, rewrite the headings first.
- Read only the first sentence of every paragraph. If that alone reads as a summary of the piece, the structure is sound. If it rambles, the paragraphs are buried.
- Mark every paragraph that could be deleted without loss. Delete it.
- Check the opening promises what the body delivers, and the body delivers what the closing claims.

## The line pass

The seven cuts, applied sentence by sentence:

1. **Adverbs of degree** - very, really, quite, extremely. Cut.
2. **Hedges** - "it could be argued", "in some cases", "it is important to note". Cut or commit.
3. **Throat-clearing** - "Before we get into it", "Let's take a look at". Cut.
4. **Redundancy** - "advance planning", "end result", "past history", "₹0 free". Cut one word.
5. **Nominalisations** - "the implementation of" becomes "implementing"; "the utilisation of" becomes "using".
6. **Double qualifiers** - "very unique", "completely essential". One, or none.
7. **The sentence that only transitions.** Cut. The reader does not need signposts between two adjacent ideas.

Then: read every sentence and ask "would I say this out loud to one person?" If not, rewrite it until you would.

## The fact-check

Build a proof table for every non-trivial piece. It is the difference between content and a liability.

| Claim in draft | Type | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| "60% of tickets were repeats" | internal metric | their Freshdesk export, Oct 2026 | verified |
| "The Indian SaaS market will reach $50B by 2030" | external stat | [VERIFY - need the report and year] | unverified |
| "Customers save 11 hours a month" | customer claim | 3 customer interviews, named | verified |
| "We are the largest in India" | superlative | no source | cut |

Rules:

- **Every number needs a source, a date and a unit.** "Revenue grew" is not a claim. "Revenue grew 34% year on year to ₹4.2 crore in FY26, per the audited accounts" is.
- **Superlatives need proof or they go.** "Largest", "fastest", "leading", "#1" are legal exposure in most markets, and hollow to a reader.
- **Quotes are verbatim or they are not quotes.** If you tightened a customer's words, call it a paraphrase or get approval.
- **Dates and time zones** - "last year" is a landmine. Write the year.
- **Currency and units** - state the currency, use the market's grouping (₹1,49,999), and never mix lakh and million in one piece.
- **Causal claims need more than correlation.** "Our clients grew 40% after working with us" invites "compared to what?". Say what the comparison is or soften the claim honestly.
- **Anything still unverified at publication is cut, not softened.** Softening keeps the risk and loses the value.

## Consistency checks before publishing

- **Names** - one spelling per person and company, checked against their own site or signature.
- **Numbers** - the same figure appears identically everywhere, including the headline, the body and the graphic.
- **Tense and person** - not switching between "we" and "the company" mid-piece.
- **Style choices** - one form of each: `e.g.` vs `for example`, sentence case vs title case in headings, Oxford comma or not.
- **Links** - every one opens, points to the right page, and has descriptive text rather than "click here".
- **Dates in the future** - a piece written in October that says "next month" is wrong by November. Use absolute dates.
- **Disclaimers and disclosures** - present, legible, and where the client's policy or the platform requires them.

## Anti-patterns

- Editing copy while the structure is still wrong. The most common wasted hour.
- Fact-checking from memory. If you did not open the source in this session, it is unverified.
- Rounding a number to make it sound better, then reporting it as the real figure.
- Keeping a claim because it is central to the argument. Central and wrong is the worst combination.
- Adding "reportedly" to a claim you have not checked. It moves the risk to the reader without removing it.
- Editing to a house style that the client has never agreed to.
- Cutting so hard the piece loses its warmth. Terse is not the only kind of clear.
