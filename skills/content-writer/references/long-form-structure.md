# Long-form structure

A long piece is not a short piece made longer. It is an argument with a spine, and the reader must be able to feel the spine on every screen.

## The four long-form shapes

| Shape | Use when | Spine |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem → cost → mechanism → proof → action** | Blog post, landing page, sales one-pager | The reader's pain is real, expensive, and has a specific fix |
| **Claim → objection → evidence → so what** | Opinion post, thought leadership | One contestable sentence defended honestly |
| **Story → turn → lesson → application** | Case study, founder post, newsletter | A real sequence of events with a change in the middle |
| **How-to → steps → traps → checklist** | Tutorial, guide, documentation | A task the reader can complete today |

Pick one before outlining. Mixing two produces the piece that reads well and goes nowhere.

## Outlining in claims

Every heading should be a sentence the reader believes by the end of that section. Test: read only the headings. Do they make an argument?

```
Bad outline (topics)          Good outline (claims)
- Introduction                - The 40-minute weekly report is a symptom, not a problem
- The problem                 - Most teams automate the report instead of the decision
- Our solution                - Three inputs decide 90% of it
- Features                    - Here is the one automation that pays for itself in a month
- Conclusion                  - Start with the decision, not the data
```

## Length by placement

| Placement | Target | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Blog post / article | 900-1600 words | Below 800 needs a very sharp point; above 2000 needs sub-sections and a summary |
| Landing page | 400-800 words | Every section is a scroll-stop; no section longer than 3 sentences |
| Case study | 600-1000 words | Before, intervention, after with numbers, quote, what did not work |
| Sales one-pager | 300-500 words | Problem, mechanism, proof, price or next step. One page, literally |
| Newsletter issue | 400-900 words | One main idea, one link set, one ask |
| White paper | 2500-5000 words | Needs a real methodology section or it is a brochure |

## Opening lines that work

- **The specific scene.** "In March, a Coimbatore textile exporter spent 11 hours rebuilding the same invoice spreadsheet."
- **The number that reframes.** "Ninety percent of the support tickets we handled last quarter were answered by the help page nobody had written."
- **The contradiction.** "The busiest month in our sales calendar is also the month we make the least money."
- **The plain admission.** "We got this wrong for eighteen months."
- **The reader's own sentence.** "You already have the data. You just cannot get it out."

Openings that do not work: a definition, a dictionary quote, "since the dawn of time", "in today's competitive landscape", a paragraph about the company.

## Body mechanics

- **Sub-sections every 250-350 words** on anything over 800. Screens are short.
- **One example per claim.** An abstract claim followed by an abstract explanation is two claims and no proof.
- **Front-load each paragraph.** The point goes in the first sentence; the support follows.
- **Vary paragraph length.** A two-line paragraph after three long ones resets attention.
- **Use a table when the content is a comparison**, a list when it is a sequence, prose when it is an argument. Do not use bullets to avoid deciding what the argument is.
- **Name the reader's objection before they do.** "You might be thinking this only works at scale. Here is the smallest case we have seen it work at."

## Endings

- **Land on the action or the reframe.** Never on a summary of what they just read.
- **One CTA.** Two CTAs halve both.
- **The last line should be quotable** - if someone screenshots one line, it should be this one.
- **Do not add "in conclusion".** The reader knows.

## Anti-patterns

- The warm-up paragraph. Delete it; the real opening is usually the second one.
- Sections that exist to balance the piece ("Challenges" and "Conclusion") rather than to advance it.
- A 2000-word piece with three ideas, each of which deserved 600 words and its own piece.
- Headings used as decoration, with no argument in them.
- Features listed as benefits. "256-bit encryption" is a feature; "your customer list stays yours" is the benefit.
- A CTA that asks for something the piece did not earn.
- Padding to hit a word count. If the idea is 700 words, publish 700.
