# Speaker notes and delivery

The notes are not a transcript. They are the three things the presenter needs on this slide: what to say, what to do with their hands and eyes, and what question is coming.

## What goes in the notes for each slide

| Field | Content | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Opening line | The first ten words out of their mouth | One sentence |
| The point | The claim in the presenter's own words | One sentence |
| The number to say aloud | The one figure that must be heard, not read | One number |
| The transition | The sentence that takes them to the next slide | One sentence |
| Skip if short | What can be dropped without breaking the argument | One line |
| The question this invites | The likely challenge, and the honest answer | One line |

## Writing notes in the presenter's voice

- Use the words they already use in meetings. If they say "burn", do not write "cash consumption".
- Keep sentences speakable: under 20 words, no subordinate clauses.
- Write numbers the way they will be said: "four point two crore", not "₹4,20,00,000".
- Mark emphasis with capitals, not with bold - bold is invisible on a lectern.

## Timing

- **Rehearse to time, once, out loud.** Reading silently takes 60% of the time speaking takes.
- **Mark a hard stop slide** - the slide they must reach by minute 10. If they are behind it, they skip.
- **The ask happens before the last two minutes.** Questions eat time, and the ask cannot be the casualty.
- **Prepare a 3-minute version.** Every meeting compresses: a 15-minute slot becomes 5 when the previous item overran.

## What to cut when running late

In this order: the second and third evidence slides, the team slide, the market detail, the history. Never cut the complication and never cut the ask.

## Handling questions

| Question type | The move |
|---|---|
| "What's your CAC?" | Answer with the number and its basis, then stop. Do not add two more numbers |
| "Who else is doing this?" | Name them, name the difference in one sentence, and do not disparage |
| A question you cannot answer | "I don't know - I'll have that to you by Thursday." Then actually do it |
| A hostile question | Repeat it back in neutral words, answer the real concern, not the tone |
| A question that is really an objection | Answer it as an objection: acknowledge, evidence, then the ask again |
| Three questions at once | Answer the one about money first, and say which you are answering |

## The appendix is the answer machine

Put the detail there and know where it is. "That's slide 34" is a strong answer; "we can send it later" is a weak one. Build the appendix from the questions the last three meetings actually produced.

## Delivery basics that matter more than the slides

- Look at the room for the title line of each slide; look at the slide only when pointing at the number.
- Pause after the complication. It is the only silence the audience will remember as confidence.
- Do not read the slides aloud. The room reads faster than the presenter speaks, which is why the notes must add, not repeat.
- Stand still for the ask. Movement at the ask reads as doubt.
- Have the one number memorised. Fumbling for a figure on your own slide costs more credibility than any design flaw.

## Anti-patterns

- **A full script in the notes.** The presenter reads it, the delivery flattens, and the audience can tell.
- **No notes at all**, then a 40-slide deck delivered by scrolling.
- **"Any questions?" as the close.** The close is the ask; questions come after.
- **Rehearsing only in the head.** Silent rehearsal always runs 40% short of the real thing.
- **Skipping the rehearsal because the deck changed.** The deck always changes; rehearse the final version once.
