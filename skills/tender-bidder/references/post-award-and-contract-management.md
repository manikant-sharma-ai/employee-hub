# Post-award and contract management

The bid is the cheap half. The order is where the money is made or given back.

## The first fourteen days after award

| Day | Task | Consequence if skipped |
|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | Read the order copy against the bid; note every difference | A term you did not bid is a cost you did not price |
| 3-5 | PBG arranged and submitted in the required format | Order cancelled, EMD forfeited |
| 5-7 | Agreement signed, stamp duty per the state | Unenforceable terms, and a payment dispute later |
| 7-10 | Delivery schedule confirmed with production and logistics | LD begins on a date nobody planned for |
| 10-14 | Buyer's contact, inspection agency and payment process confirmed | Inspection delays become LD |
| 14 | First progress note to the buyer | Silence is read as risk |

## The delivery, tracked weekly

Track four things, every week, in one sheet: production status against the schedule, dispatch and
transit, inspection status, and payment status against the invoice. The LD clock starts at the
contractual delivery date, not at the date someone remembers.

## Inspection and acceptance

- Know the inspection agency and the sampling plan before dispatch.
- Keep the test certificates, batch numbers and packing list ready at the point of inspection.
- A failed inspection is usually a documentation failure as much as a quality one.
- Record the acceptance date; it starts the warranty and, often, the payment clock.

## Payment

Government payment cycles are long and documented. Invoice in the exact format the order specifies,
with the GST details, the PO reference, the inspection certificate and the delivery proof. Follow up
on a schedule: 15 days after submission, then every 15, in writing, with the invoice number. Where a
payment is stuck, the escalation route is written in the contract - use it, politely, in writing.

## Ratings and the next bid

On GeM, the buyer rates the order. A poor rating follows the seller across categories and can restrict
participation. The rating is driven by delivery on time, the item matching the specification, and how
the seller handled a problem. A single late order handled with a call and a revised schedule scores
better than silence.

## Repeat business, which is the real prize

A government buyer who has received a good order is the cheapest customer in the business. Two things
create the repeat: the completion certificate (ask for it at acceptance, not a year later) and the
buyer's knowledge that the seller answers the phone. Keep the completion certificate in the standing
pack; it is the document every future eligibility test asks for.

## Anti-patterns

- **Signing the order without reading it against the bid.** The order is the contract.
- **Late PBG.** The most common avoidable cancellation in public procurement.
- **Dispatch without the inspection clearance.** Rejected at the buyer's gate, at your freight cost.
- **Invoice format improvised.** A returned invoice costs a month of payment cycle.
- **No completion certificate.** The performance is invisible to every future tender.
- **Treating a poor rating as unfair.** It is a data point the next buyer reads before your bid.

## The contract file

One folder per order, and it is what saves the business at the first payment dispute:

| Document | Why it is kept |
|---|---|
| The bid as submitted | Proves what was offered |
| The order copy and the agreement | The contract of record |
| The PBG and its validity | Release depends on it, and it is forgotten |
| Inspection reports and test certificates | Acceptance evidence |
| Dispatch documents, e-way bill, proof of delivery | Payment and LD evidence |
| Invoices with the PO reference | The payment trail |
| Correspondence, including every reminder | The escalation record |
| Completion certificate | The eligibility proof for the next tender |

A file that is complete at acceptance is worth more than any argument made a year later.
