# Objection handling

An objection is information, not rejection. The buyer is telling you what has to be true before they can say yes.

## The structure, every time

1. **Acknowledge in their words.** Not "I understand your concern" - repeat the objection back accurately enough that they nod.
2. **Separate the stated objection from the real one.** Price usually means value; timing usually means priority; "send me info" usually means "I do not want to talk to you yet".
3. **Answer with evidence**, not with a counter-claim.
4. **Ask a question that moves the decision.** A yes/no next step, or a diagnostic question that surfaces the real constraint.
5. **Take the no if it is a no.** A clean no today is a warm contact in two quarters.

## The eight objections that carry most of the volume

| Objection | What it usually means | Answer shape | Walk away when |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Too expensive" | They cannot see the return, or have no budget line | Convert to cost of the status quo per month, compare to the price; offer the smaller entry scope | Price is below their cost of change and they still object |
| "Send me information" | Polite deferral | Send one page, then ask one diagnostic question with a date | After two rounds with no question answered |
| "We already have a vendor" | Switching cost is invisible to them | Ask what happens at renewal, and what one thing they would change | They are contractually locked with no exit date |
| "Not the right person" | Real routing | Ask for the one name and permission to mention them; ask what that person cares about | They will not name anyone after two asks |
| "We're too small / too big for this" | Fit doubt | Name the smallest and largest customer already using it, with the number | Fit genuinely does not exist - do not force it |
| "No budget this year" | Timing, not value | Ask when the budget cycle starts and what would have to change | Two budget cycles pass with no change |
| "We tried something like this and it failed" | Prior wound | Ask what happened; name the specific reason this is different, or admit it is not | The failure was the same mechanism and it is unchanged |
| "Just email me in six months" | Closing the door politely | Get one date and one trigger, then honour it exactly | No date, no trigger - close it |

## Price, done properly

Never discount to end an objection; you teach the buyer that the first price was fiction.

| Step | What to say |
|---|---|
| 1. Quantify the status quo | "What does one stock-out cost you in a month - in lost orders or in expedited freight?" |
| 2. Let them do the arithmetic | Silence after the question is a tool. Wait. |
| 3. Compare against the annual price | "So ₹6 lakh a year of stock-outs against ₹1.2 lakh of software." |
| 4. Offer scope, not discount | Fewer modules, one site, a paid pilot with a defined end |
| 5. Name what the pilot proves | "If reconciliation does not fall below 4 days in 60 days, you walk and keep the data." |

## "Send me information"

The most common dead end in outbound. Handle it in three lines:

> Of course. I'll send one page - the Pune case with the actual numbers.
>
> So I send the right thing: is the problem multi-site counting, or the audit trail for your OEM contracts?
>
> If it is neither, say so and I'll stop.

That converts a brush-off into a diagnostic question and gives them an honourable exit, which is why it gets answered.

## Language patterns that lower resistance

- "Fair enough." - acknowledges without arguing.
- "Most people in your position say the same thing, and here is what changed their mind." - normalises the objection.
- "What would have to be true for this to be worth doing?" - moves from objection to conditions.
- "Am I wrong that...?" - invites correction rather than pushing.
- "That's a no for this year, then?" - gives permission to decline, which often produces the real reason.

## Language patterns that raise it

- "I completely understand, however..." - the "however" deletes the understanding.
- "With respect..." - never read as respectful.
- "Our solution is best-in-class" - a claim with no evidence, at the moment evidence is required.
- "Everybody else is doing it" - a pressure tactic that makes a considered buyer defensive.
- Answering a question they did not ask, at length.

## Anti-patterns

- **Rehearsing a script instead of listening.** The objection in the reply is often not the objection in the reference file.
- **Overcoming an objection that was not an objection.** "Thanks, noted" needs no rebuttal.
- **Treating "no" as a negotiating position** when it is a decision. You lose the reference, not just the deal.
- **Arguing with the stated price** before understanding the budget cycle.
- **Inventing a customer reference** to answer a sceptical question. One check by the buyer ends the relationship permanently.
- **Following up on a closed objection** without new information. It reads as not having listened.
