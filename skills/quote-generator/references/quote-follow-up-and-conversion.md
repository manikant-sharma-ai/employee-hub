# Follow-up and conversion

A quote that is not followed up is a price disclosed to a competitor for free. This file is the
sequence, the objections and the conversion.

## The follow-up rhythm

| When | Channel | What to say |
|---|---|---|
| Same day | Email with the quote | What is included, the validity date, the next step |
| Day 2 | WhatsApp or phone | "Did it reach you, and is the specification right?" - a confirmation, not a chase |
| Day 5 | Phone | The one question: what is stopping the decision |
| Day 10 | Email | A specific answer to the objection heard, or a revision if scope changed |
| Day 14 | Phone | The validity reminder, and a decision date request |
| Day 20-25 | Email | The final validity note, and what happens after it lapses |
| After validity | Email | The revised quote, with the reason for the change |

Three contacts is not a chase; seven is. The line is whether each contact carries new information.

## The five objections and what each one is really about

| Objection | Usually means | The response |
|---|---|---|
| "Too expensive" | No value case, or a genuine budget gap | Break the price into what is included; offer a smaller scope, not a discount |
| "Competitor is cheaper" | Comparing different scopes | Ask for the specification; compare line by line, and concede a row where they are right |
| "Need to think" | An unanswered question inside their organisation | Ask who else decides, and what they will ask |
| "Send your best price" | A negotiation ritual, or a mandate to get three quotes | Ask what price would close it, and what changes at that price |
| "Not this quarter" | A real budget cycle | Ask for the date it reopens, and diarise it |

## Revisions, versioned

Every revision carries: a new number or a version suffix, the date, the change, and the reason. Keep
the old one. When a customer says "you quoted us X", the answer is a document, not a memory.

## Conversion: quote to order to invoice

1. **Purchase order received** - check it against the quote: items, specification, quantity, price,
   taxes, delivery, payment terms, penalty clauses.
2. **Differences** - resolve in writing before acknowledgement. The PO is the contract.
3. **Acknowledgement** - confirm the order, the price, the delivery date and the payment terms.
4. **Proforma invoice** where an advance is due.
5. **Handover to delivery** - production, dispatch, installation, each with the date.
6. **Handover to invoicing** - `finance-ops` raises the tax invoice against the accepted PO, with the
   same numbers.

## Win/loss, recorded

Two fields per quote, always: the outcome and the reason. After twenty quotes, the reasons cluster, and
the cluster is the finding - usually one of price against a different scope, lead time, or a missing
certification. Without the record, every loss is "they were cheaper" and nothing improves.

## Anti-patterns

- **Following up with "just checking in".** It adds nothing and trains the customer to ignore you.
- **Discounting at the first objection.** It confirms the first price was not real.
- **Accepting a PO without checking it against the quote.** The terms change there.
- **A revision with no version number.** Two prices, one reference, a dispute.
- **No win/loss reason.** The same loss repeats next quarter with a different customer.
- **Chasing a dead quote for a month.** Two follow-ups after validity, then close it and say so.

## The handover to delivery

Winning the order is the start of the risk. On acknowledgement, hand over four things in writing:

1. **The accepted specification** - item, variant, quantity, exactly as quoted.
2. **The delivery commitment** - date, place, basis (ex-works or FOR), and who unloads.
3. **The payment schedule** - what is due when, and against which document.
4. **The exclusions** - the civil work, the power point, the statutory approval, so nobody assumes.

Then confirm the same four with the customer in the acknowledgement. A handover that repeats the quote
is what stops the "we thought installation was included" conversation three weeks later.
