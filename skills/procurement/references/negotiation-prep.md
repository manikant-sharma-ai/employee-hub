# Negotiation prep

Negotiation is not a personality trait. It is four numbers written down before the
call, and a ladder of concessions you decided in advance.

## The four numbers

| Number | How to get it | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Target | What the market says is fair - from other quotes, a rate card, a similar past purchase | Your opening ask |
| Walk-away | The point beyond which another vendor, a repair, a lease or doing without is better | Without it you have no position |
| Their floor | Their likely cost plus a thin margin - from their scale, their raw material, their utilisation | Tells you how far the room extends |
| Your ceiling | Budget approved, and the TCO that still works | Stops a win that loses money |

## The levers, in order of what vendors will actually move

1. **Volume or commitment.** An annual commitment with a monthly draw beats a
   one-off order at the same total.
2. **Payment terms.** Advance or shorter terms are worth real money to a small
   vendor; ask for 3-6% for paying in 7 days.
3. **Scope.** Remove installation, take 12 months of consumables, drop the training
   - the same vendor can often move 8% by changing what is in the box.
4. **Price.** The last lever, not the first, and the one with the least room.
5. **Timing.** Off-season, quarter-end or a slow month is worth 5-10% with vendors
   who have fixed costs.
6. **A reference or a case study.** Worth more to a growing vendor than the margin.

## The concession ladder

Decide in advance what you will give, in what order, and what you get for each:

| You give | You ask for | Never give |
|---|---|---|
| A 12-month commitment | 8% off the rate card | A commitment with no exit |
| Payment in 7 days | 3% discount | Payment before delivery |
| A larger first order | A lower unit price on the whole order | Stock you cannot use |
| A named reference | Extended warranty | A reference without the customer's consent |
| Flexibility on delivery dates | A price held for 90 days | A price that expires after you have committed |

## The opening sentence

Bad: "What is your best price?" - invites a token discount and a lost benchmark.

Better: "We have three quotes. Yours is second on price and first on service. If
you can reach ₹3.85 landed with 45-day payment, we will place the annual order
this week."

That sentence contains the benchmark, the reason they are in the running, the
specific number, and the thing you are offering.

## What not to do

- **Do not reveal your budget.** It becomes the price.
- **Do not negotiate with three vendors simultaneously in the room.** Run them in
  sequence and let each know a decision is imminent, without naming the others' prices.
- **Do not bluff about a quote you do not have.** It gets tested.
- **Do not negotiate the price before the specification is fixed.** You will win a
  discount and lose the feature.
- **Do not accept "we will adjust it later"** on a delivery date or a warranty.

## When the vendor will not move

Three honest outcomes:

1. **Take it** - the TCO is still the best, and the difference is smaller than the
   cost of switching. Say so.
2. **Change the scope** - remove something, and get the price to the target.
3. **Walk** - and mean it. A walk-away that is never used is not a walk-away, and
   the vendor learns that by the second renewal.

## After the call

Write the agreed terms into a one-page confirmation and send it the same day:
price, validity, scope, delivery, payment, warranty, service response. Verbal
agreements become different agreements by the time the PO is raised.

## Anti-patterns

- Opening with "best price" and accepting the first counter.
- A walk-away number invented in the meeting.
- Winning 6% and losing 12 months of warranty.
- Negotiating with the salesperson when the decision sits with the proprietor.
- Treating a discount as the only outcome; terms and scope often beat it.
- No written confirmation, so the price "agreed" is later "misunderstood".
