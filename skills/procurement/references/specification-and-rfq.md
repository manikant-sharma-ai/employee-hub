# Specification and RFQ

The specification is where procurement is won or lost. A vague requirement
produces five quotes for five different things, and the cheapest one wins by
accident.

## The one-page requirement

| Block | Content | Test |
|---|---|---|
| The job | What the thing must do, in one sentence, outcome not brand | A stranger can repeat it |
| Volume | Units per hour, per month, per year - and the peak | The peak, not the average, sizes the machine |
| Conditions | Power, floor space, temperature, dust, water, operator skill | Site realities, not brochure conditions |
| Must-haves | Three to five, each binary | "Must hold ±0.5 mm", not "good accuracy" |
| Scored preferences | Three to five, each with a weight | Warranty years, service response, spare availability |
| Exclusions | What is explicitly out of scope | Installation, training, electrical work, GST |
| Acceptance | How the thing is proved to work, and by when | A test, a sample run, a tolerance measurement |

## Must-have versus nice-to-have

- A **must-have** disqualifies a bid when absent. Keep it to five; nine filters
  leave one bidder and no negotiating room.
- A **preference** is scored 0-5 and weighted. It never disqualifies.
- Anything that cannot be measured is not a criterion. "Reputation" is measured by
  called references; "quality" by a sample test.

## Writing an RFQ that produces comparable quotes

| Section | What to include |
|---|---|
| Scope | What is bought, what is not, and who does installation |
| Volumes | Annual quantity and the monthly pattern, with the peak month named |
| Price schedule | A fixed table for them to fill: unit price, freight, installation, GST rate, warranty cost |
| Terms | Payment terms offered, delivery lead time required, warranty period required |
| Compliance | GSTIN, PAN, MSME/Udyam status, statutory certifications for the category |
| References | Two customers of the same size and use, with phone numbers |
| Submission | Format, deadline, validity of the quote (60-90 days), and who to send it to |

The price schedule is the single most useful device: when every vendor fills the
same rows, the comparison is arithmetic instead of interpretation.

## Recurring versus one-off

| Buying | Structure | Term |
|---|---|---|
| Consumables, packaging, courier | Annual rate contract with a monthly draw | 12 months, price review quarterly or on an index |
| Capital equipment | One RFQ, three bidders, installation milestone | Warranty 12-24 months, AMC after |
| Services, contract labour | Rate card per role or per output, with a headcount cap | 12 months, 30-day notice |
| Software | Per-seat or usage, with the renewal cap written | 12 months, cap on renewal increase |
| Emergency breakdown | Single source, written reason, premium disclosed | Immediate, review within 30 days |

## The renewal trap

Every recurring purchase gets three lines in the contract negotiation, even when
the first price is good:

1. **A cap on the renewal increase** - the lower of 8% or the WPI change for the
   category, in writing.
2. **A notice period** - 60 days before auto-renewal, so the review happens.
3. **An exit** - what happens to stock, deposits, data and transition support.

Without these, the first-year price is a discount and the second-year price is the
real one.

## Anti-patterns

- Sending a WhatsApp "send me your best rate" and calling the replies quotes.
- Letting the vendor draft the specification, then buying their model.
- A must-have list of twelve items, which guarantees a single bidder.
- Comparing prices without stating whether GST is included.
- No acceptance test, so a machine that "works" is installed and does not.
- Ignoring the peak volume and buying for the average.
- No quote validity date, so a March price is assumed in November.
