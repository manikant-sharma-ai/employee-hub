# Synthesis and the decision memo

Gathering is the easy half. This file is how evidence becomes a decision someone can act on, in one page, without losing the truth in the compression.

## Synthesis: from notes to structure

1. **Dump every finding on one surface**, one per line, each with its confidence label.
2. **Group by what it implies**, not by where it came from. "Buyers are price-anchored" is a group; "findings from interviews" is not.
3. **Kill the duplicates.** Ten findings that say the same thing are one finding with ten sources. Merge them and keep the strongest source.
4. **Mark the load-bearing three.** Which three findings, if wrong, would change the recommendation? Everything else is supporting colour.
5. **Write the answer in one sentence before writing the memo.** If you cannot, you have not decided anything yet - and that is a finding too.

## The one-page memo structure

Fixed order, because the reader decides in the first twenty seconds.

```
1. The decision question      - one line, with actor, amount, date
2. The answer                 - one paragraph, recommendation first
3. Why (three reasons)        - the load-bearing three, each with evidence
4. What it costs / needs      - money, people, time, the constraint
5. What would change my mind  - three to five observable conditions
6. The counter-case           - the strongest argument against, stated fairly
7. What I could not find      - named gaps and the risk each carries
8. Next cheapest step         - the smallest action that reduces uncertainty most
```

Then the appendix: evidence table, sources with dates, the model with its assumptions, interview notes.

## Writing the recommendation

| Bad | Good | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "The market looks promising" | "Enter Pune in Q1 with two bays" | An action |
| "There are several options" | "Option B, because the unit economics work at 40% occupancy where A needs 65%" | A choice with a reason |
| "It depends on execution" | "It depends on securing one anchor contract; without it, do not proceed" | Names the dependency |
| "Further research is needed" | "₹1.2 lakh of paid buyer interviews would settle the pricing question; nothing else will" | Scopes the gap |
| "I recommend we proceed cautiously" | "Proceed with a ₹4 lakh pilot in one city for 90 days, with a stop condition at 30 orders" | Cautious, and specific |

A recommendation must be **falsifiable**: a reader should be able to say what outcome would prove it wrong. If they cannot, you have written an opinion with a table attached.

## Handling genuine uncertainty

Three honest moves, in order of preference:

1. **Quantify it.** "The answer flips if the landed cost exceeds ₹118 a unit; our range is ₹104-131, so this is a coin flip."
2. **Buy it down cheaply.** Name the single cheapest test that would resolve it, with a cost and a duration.
3. **Recommend a reversible version.** Pilot, trial order, staged rollout, option to exit. This is the right answer more often than a binary yes/no.

Never resolve uncertainty by choosing the more confident-sounding number.

## Options analysis

When the decision is a choice between options, use one table and no prose padding.

| Option | What it needs | Cost | Time to first evidence | Downside if wrong | Reversible? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | | | | | |
| B | | | | | |
| Do nothing | | | | | |

"Reversible?" is the column founders under-use. A reversible option with slightly worse expected value usually beats an irreversible one with slightly better.

## The pre-mortem

Before the memo goes out, spend ten minutes here:

> It is twelve months later and this failed. What happened?

Write the top five causes. For each, one line on whether it is detectable now. Causes that are detectable now and unaddressed belong in the memo as risks, not in your head.

## Compression without distortion

The memo is one page; the evidence is not. The failure modes of compression:

- **Dropping the caveat** because it made the sentence longer. Put the caveat in the "what would change my mind" section instead of losing it.
- **Rounding a range to a point** to sound decisive. Keep the range; state the midpoint and the spread.
- **Presenting an assumption as an input.** Label it in the table.
- **Turning "we could not verify" into silence.** Silence reads as verified.

## Presenting it

- **Lead with the answer.** No build-up, no "as requested, I have researched...".
- **One page, then stop.** Offer the appendix; do not walk through it.
- **Name the two decisions it affects most.** It helps the reader place it.
- **Say the uncomfortable part yourself**, before they find it. A memo that hides its own weakness is discovered, not read.

## Anti-patterns

- A memo that summarises the research instead of answering the question.
- Burying the recommendation on page four.
- No counter-case section, because it felt disloyal.
- Three frameworks stacked, none of which decided anything.
- A recommendation with no cost attached. Every recommendation costs something; say what.
- "Further research is needed" as the conclusion, with no scope or price for it.
- Presenting a range of outcomes without saying which one you expect.
- Writing the memo for the person who asked rather than the person who decides.
