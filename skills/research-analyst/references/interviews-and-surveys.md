# Interviews and surveys

Desk research tells you what is published. Primary research tells you what is true
about your customer, and it is the only way to find the thing nobody wrote down.

## When primary research is worth the cost

- The decision is expensive and irreversible.
- The published sources disagree, or all come from the same interested party.
- You need the language customers use, not the language the industry uses.
- You are about to build something and the last three assumptions are guesses.

Otherwise, desk research is cheaper and usually enough.

## Interviews: six to twelve, not sixty

- **Six interviews** find most of the pattern; twelve confirms it. Sixty is a survey pretending to be qualitative.
- **Recruit across the range**, not the average: the best customer, the churned one, the one who never bought.
- **Ask about the past, never the future.** "Tell me about the last time you did X" beats "would you use Y".
- **Never pitch.** The moment you describe the product, the answers become polite.
- **Record with permission**, transcribe, and quote verbatim - the quote is the finding.

## The interview guide, five questions

1. Walk me through the last time you dealt with <problem>.
2. What did you do first? Then what?
3. What was the most annoying part?
4. What did it cost you - money, time, or something else?
5. What did you try before this, and why did it stop?

## Surveys: for counting, not for discovering

- **Under 100 responses is an anecdote with a chart.** Say so.
- **No leading questions.** "How much do you love our service?" is a marketing asset, not research.
- **One idea per question**, no double-barrelled items.
- **Always include "prefer not to say"** on income, caste, gender and age.
- **Response rate is the finding.** A 4% response from a purchased list tells you nothing about the market.

## Sample and bias

- **Who is missing?** A survey through your Instagram audience excludes the customers who left.
- **Incentives skew toward the price-sensitive**; note the incentive in the readout.
- **Self-reported behaviour is inflated** - people overstate research, understate impulse.

## Synthesis

One page per finding: the claim, the verbatim evidence, how many respondents support
it, and the confidence. Then a single decision memo that separates what was verified
from what was assumed.

## Anti-patterns

- A 40-question survey with no hypothesis.
- Interviewing only happy customers.
- Presenting six interviews as "the market".
- A finding with no verbatim quote behind it.
- Asking "would you pay for this" and treating the yes as demand.
