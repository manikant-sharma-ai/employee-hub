# The founder's time and delegation

A founder's calendar is the real strategy. Everything else - the deck, the plan,
the offsite - is a description of what the calendar already decided.

## The monthly time audit

Take the last four weeks of calendar plus the sent mail, and bucket every hour:

| Bucket | What belongs there |
|---|---|
| Selling | Pipeline, calls, proposals, pricing conversations |
| Delivering | Customer work, support escalations, project firefighting |
| Building | Product, content, systems, hiring |
| Running | Approvals, admin, finance reviews, vendor calls |
| Interrupted | Unplanned, low-value, someone else's urgency |
| Thinking | Unbroken blocks for the decisions only the founder can make |

Then compare the split to the quarter's priorities. In most small businesses,
"Running" and "Interrupted" together exceed half the week, and the priorities get
the leftovers.

## The two jobs only the founder can do

For most businesses: **deciding what the company does next**, and **the
relationships that cannot be delegated** - the largest customers, the key
investors, the co-founder conversations. Everything else has an owner or can.

The test for any task: would this be done 80% as well by someone else with the
right brief? If yes, it is not founder work.

## The delegation ladder

| Level | The founder does | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Do it yourself | The task | Nothing, except the founder's time |
| 2. Do it and show me | The task, then the founder reviews | Slow, but safe |
| 3. Recommend and I approve | The person proposes, the founder decides | The default for most work |
| 4. Decide and tell me | The person decides, informs the founder | Requires clear boundaries |
| 5. Own it | The person owns the outcome and the reporting | Requires trust and a metric |

Delegation fails at the jump from 1 to 5. Move one level at a time, and write down
which level each recurring decision sits at.

## The handover brief

What a founder must give before letting go of a task:

1. **The outcome** - what good looks like, with a number.
2. **The boundaries** - what the person may decide alone, what needs approval, what is off-limits.
3. **The cadence** - when they report, and in what format.
4. **The first example** - one thing the founder did, so the standard is visible.
5. **The failure rule** - what to do when it goes wrong, and when to escalate.

## Protecting the deep-work block

- **Two blocks of 90 minutes a week**, before 11:00, in the calendar as a meeting with yourself.
- **No meetings before 11:00 on one day a week**, if the business can bear it.
- **Batch approvals into two windows a day.** Every approval interrupts a thought.
- **One "no" list** - the recurring requests the founder will decline by default, and who handles them instead.

## Anti-patterns

- **Delegating the task but keeping the anxiety.** Reviewing every detail teaches nobody anything.
- **Taking the work back at the first mistake.** The person learns the task is not really theirs.
- **Hiring a generalist to absorb chaos.** Without a defined outcome, the hire becomes an assistant to the founder's inbox.
- **The 60-hour week as a badge.** It usually means the business is being run by interruption.
- **Delegating without a metric.** A task with no number cannot be handed over, only watched.
