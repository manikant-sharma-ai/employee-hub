# The decision log and reversibility

The same decision taken four times is a symptom: nobody wrote the first one down,
including why.

## The record

| Field | Why it exists |
|---|---|
| Date | The anchor for every later "when did we decide this" |
| Decision | One sentence, in the affirmative: "we will not take custom orders below 50 units" |
| Context | The two or three facts that made it necessary |
| Options rejected | The alternative and the reason - this is what stops the re-argument |
| Owner | Who is accountable for it holding |
| Reversal test | What evidence would make us change it, and by when we would look |
| Review date | When it is revisited regardless |
| Status | Active, superseded, reversed |

Two fields do most of the work: **options rejected** and **reversal test**. Without
them the log is a diary; with them it is a decision system.

## Reversible versus irreversible

| Type | Cost of being wrong | How to decide |
|---|---|---|
| Reversible, cheap | Hours or a small spend | Decide today, by whoever is closest, and log it |
| Reversible, expensive | Weeks or a real spend | Decide this week, with the two options written down |
| Irreversible | A hire, a lease, a co-founder, a brand change, a public commitment | Write it, sleep on it, decide with a date and a reversal test |

The commonest small-business failure is treating reversible decisions as
irreversible - three weeks of deliberation over a landing page - while treating an
irreversible one as reversible: a hire made in a week, a lease signed on a call.

## Deciding fast without being reckless

Three questions, ninety seconds:

1. **What is the cost of being wrong, in money and weeks?**
2. **Can we reverse it, and at what cost?**
3. **Who has the information to decide, and is that the person deciding?**

If the answer to 3 is "not the person in the room", the decision is being taken at
the wrong level, and the log should say so.

## The decision review, quarterly

Read the log for the quarter and answer:

- Which decisions were reversed, and did the reversal test predict it?
- Which decisions are still "active" but clearly not being followed?
- Which decisions were taken twice because the first was never written?
- What did we decide that nobody can now explain?

A decision that is not being followed is either a bad decision or an unenforced
one. Both are information, and neither is visible without the log.

## Writing a reversal test that works

Bad: "review in six months".
Good: "if the average order value of custom orders stays above ₹18,000 for two
consecutive months, we reopen the question on 1 March".

The test needs a metric, a threshold, a duration and a date. Anything vaguer is a
promise to remember, which is not a mechanism.

## Anti-patterns

- **The log as a transcript.** Minutes are not decisions; a page of discussion with
  no one-sentence decision is the failure this file exists to prevent.
- **The founder-only log.** Decisions taken by a manager and never logged are
  re-litigated with the founder, who then contradicts the manager in public.
- **The decision with no owner.** A decision nobody owns is a preference.
- **The silent reversal.** Changing course without logging the reversal teaches the
  team that the log is fiction.
- **The log nobody can search.** One file, one table, one naming convention, or it
  will not be read.
- **Deciding by seniority rather than by information.** The most expensive decisions
  in a small business are the ones taken by the busiest person with the least data.
